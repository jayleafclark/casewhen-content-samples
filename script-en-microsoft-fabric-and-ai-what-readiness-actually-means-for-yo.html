[1:05–2:35] Beat 1 — "What does Fabric AI readiness actually mean for your reports?"

[MODALITY: screen-record, live Microsoft Fabric with the Copilot panel open. ON-SCREEN CARD: "AI sits on top of the data you already built"]

Spoken (CLAIM): Fabric AI readiness comes down to one plain thing. When someone asks the AI a question, it can only reach data that's clean, governed, and coming from one agreed source.

[MODALITY: custom animation, the Copilot question box sitting on top of a semantic model and the OneLake data underneath it]

Spoken (PROOF): Here's the mechanism. Copilot in Fabric sits on top of the semantic model you already built and the data in OneLake underneath it. You type a plain question, it writes the query, builds the visual, and writes a short summary. It reads whatever you already made. If three of your reports define revenue three different ways, the AI inherits all three of those definitions. Ready means the data it reaches is correct, the access is set, and every number has one source.

[MODALITY: on-screen text, kinetic. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Turning AI on doesn't make your data ready. It shows how ready it already is."]

Spoken (PAYOFF): So the rule to take from this beat is simple. Turning AI on doesn't make your data ready. It reveals, fast and in front of everyone, exactly how ready your data already was.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): Which is why the same feature helps one team and embarrasses another. Let me show you how that split happens.

[2:35–4:05] Beat 2 — "Why does Fabric AI give two people two different answers?"

[MODALITY: custom animation. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Two reports. Two revenue numbers."]

Spoken (CLAIM): Most teams already define the same figure two or three ways across their reports. Fabric AI answers from whichever definition it reaches first.

[MODALITY: animation shows two report tiles, both labelled Revenue, showing different totals, and the AI pulling from each]

Spoken (PROOF): Say one report counts revenue when the order is placed and another counts it when the invoice is paid. Both are labelled Revenue. A person can look at that and know which one finance means. The AI can't. Your head of sales asks Copilot for last month's revenue and gets one total. Your head of finance asks the same question and gets a different one. Now two senior people are quoting two numbers in the same meeting, and both of them think the AI backed them up.

[MODALITY: talking head. ON-SCREEN CARD: "AI repeats your definitions. It doesn't agree them for you."]

Spoken (PAYOFF): So the takeaway you can use this afternoon. Before AI, two disagreeing numbers were a slow annoyance you sorted out privately over email. After AI, they surface instantly, with a confident written summary attached, in the review you least want to get wrong.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): That tells you where the real work sits. It's underneath the question box. So how ready is most companies' data, really? There's a number on this, and it's sobering.

[4:05–5:35] Beat 3 — "How much of your data is actually ready for AI?"

[MODALITY: data-viz, a single animated figure counting up to 12%. ON-SCREEN CARD: "12% say their data is good enough for AI"]

Spoken (CLAIM): Almost every company wants AI on its reports. Very few have the data underneath in the shape AI needs.

[MODALITY: talking head over the figure]

Spoken (PROOF): Only about 12 percent of organisations say their data is in good enough shape, both quality and access, to actually use for AI. That's from Drexel LeBow and Precisely in 2025. Look at that for a second. Nearly nine in ten teams switching on AI features are switching them on over data they themselves don't fully trust. And that tracks with a second finding, that 67 percent of organisations say they don't fully trust the data they already use to make decisions. AI doesn't change that math. It sits right on top of it.

[MODALITY: screen-record, a quick self-check. ON-SCREEN CARD: "3 questions to ask before AI"]

Spoken (PROOF, continued): You can self-check in about a minute. One. If two reports show the same figure, do they always match? Two. Could a new analyst open your model and understand the field names without calling someone? Three. Does every number that matters have one agreed definition that finance and sales both signed off on? If any answer is no, AI will amplify that gap in front of whoever is asking.

[MODALITY: on-screen text. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Readiness is a data question, not an AI question"]

Spoken (PAYOFF): So the takeaway is that Fabric AI readiness is a data question wearing an AI label. The work is unglamorous and it's the whole game. Clean data, clear names, one agreed definition for every number.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): And that word agreed carries the weight. Let me show you the single biggest piece of it, the one agreed number, and how teams actually get there.

[5:35–7:05] Beat 4 — "What's the one agreed number, and why does AI need it?"

[MODALITY: custom animation, many scattered definitions merging into one labelled measure. ON-SCREEN CARD: "One agreed number"]

Spoken (CLAIM): The fix that makes Fabric AI trustworthy has nothing to do with clever prompting. It comes from agreeing one definition for each number that matters, and building that definition into the model one time.

[MODALITY: screen-record, defining a single measure in the semantic model, then two reports both pointing at it]

Spoken (PROOF): Here's what that looks like in practice. You sit finance, sales, and operations in one room and you settle the definitions. What counts as an active customer. When revenue is recognised. How margin is calculated. Then each agreed definition gets written into the semantic model as one measure everyone draws from. Every report points at it. And now when anyone asks the AI for active customers, there's one possible answer, because there's one definition in the model for it to reach.

[MODALITY: talking head. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Agree it once. Build it once. Then everyone can ask freely."]

Spoken (PAYOFF): The takeaway you can act on. The value of Fabric AI is capped by how many of your core numbers are agreed and built once. Every number you pin down is a question your whole team can now ask the AI safely. Every number you leave loose is a wrong answer waiting to be quoted.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): Now, remember the check I said most teams run last? This is it, and it's the part that keeps founders up once they see it.

[7:05–8:35] Beat 5 — "Who can see what once Fabric AI can read your data?"

[MODALITY: custom animation, row-level security, the same report, two users, different rows visible. ON-SCREEN CARD: "AI can reach any number a person is allowed to reach"]

Spoken (CLAIM): Here's the one most teams run last. Fabric AI will surface any figure a person is allowed to reach. So if your access rights are loose, the AI becomes the fastest way to find that out.

[MODALITY: screen-record showing row-level security roles, then an AI query returning only the rows a user is entitled to]

Spoken (PROOF): Before AI, a salesperson would need to know exactly which report tab and which slicer to reach another region's margin, or a colleague's cost line. Loose access rights were a risk that mostly stayed asleep because nobody went looking. AI wakes it up. Someone types, what's our margin by region, and if row-level security isn't set correctly across the model and the OneLake data beneath it, the AI returns the whole picture, including the rows that person was never meant to see. The AI is behaving exactly as told. It follows the access rules you gave it, and those rules were too open.

[MODALITY: on-screen text. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Fix who can see what before you widen who can ask"]

Spoken (PAYOFF): So the takeaway, and this is the open loop I promised. Before you turn AI on for the whole company, check that row-level security is correct on every model and every dataset it can reach. AI widens who can ask a question. Your access rights decide who should get an answer. Line those up first.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): Put all of this together and you get a short list to run the week before you switch it on. Here it is.

[8:35–10:00] Beat 6 — "What should you fix before you turn AI on?"

[MODALITY: kinetic type, a numbered list building one item at a time. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Before you turn AI on"]

Spoken (CLAIM): Five checks. Run these before AI goes wide, and you skip almost every awkward moment I have described.

[MODALITY: on-screen numbered list, each line appears as it's spoken]

Spoken (PROOF): One. Get to one semantic model per subject area, so the AI reads a single clean structure. Two. Rename your fields to words a normal person would actually type. Three. Agree every core number once, with finance and sales in the room, and build it into the model. Four. Set and test row-level security on every model and every dataset the AI can reach. Five. Turn AI on for one small, ready team first, watch what it answers for a month, then widen it.

[MODALITY: b-roll, a Fabric capacity meter ticking up during a busy morning refresh. ON-SCREEN CARD: "AI queries draw on the same capacity as your refreshes"]

Spoken (PROOF, continued): One Fabric detail worth adding while you're there. AI queries draw on the same capacity as your refreshes and your reports. So right-size your Fabric capacity before a wide rollout, or an unready launch can spike your bill the same week it spikes your support tickets.

[MODALITY: talking head. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Ready data first. AI second."]

Spoken (PAYOFF): Do those and Fabric AI earns its keep, because it's answering from data you already trust. Every one of them is data work you should want done anyway, AI or no AI. The AI just raises the cost of skipping it.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): So the last question is the one you actually came here to settle. Is your Fabric setup ready for AI right now?

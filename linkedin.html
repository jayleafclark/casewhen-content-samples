Day 1 · MonENAustinazure data factory
Most teams build Azure Data Factory wrong, and the bill proves it.
Here's what people miss. When you drop a Mapping Data Flow into a pipeline, Azure Data Factory spins up a Spark cluster behind it, billed per vCore-hour, with a cold start that can take four to five minutes before a single row moves. Plenty of teams use one for a join a stored procedure would've finished in seconds. Then there's the sprawl. I've opened factories with 60-plus near-identical pipelines, one per source table, all copy-pasted. A single parameterized pipeline driven by a control table does the same work. When a column changes, you fix it once instead of sixty times. And the part your CFO feels most: Azure Data Factory bills you three ways at once. Activity runs, data movement measured in Data Integration Units, and any data flow compute. Teams watch the first number, ignore the third, and the data flow clusters run up the bill on their own. Bad pipelines produce bad data too, and Gartner puts the average cost of poor data quality at 12.9 million dollars a year. None of this means the tool's bad. Azure Data Factory is a strong orchestrator. The problem is teams reach for the expensive pieces first and skip the boring design work that keeps the whole thing cheap and predictable. Parameterize your pipelines, size your integration runtime, and only pull in data flows when you truly need Spark.

Day 2 · TueENAustinazure synapse analytics
Most teams get Azure Synapse Analytics wrong before the first query even runs.
Here's what I keep seeing. A team buys Azure Synapse Analytics to put the data lake and the warehouse in one place, spins up a dedicated SQL pool, and then that pool sits there billing at full DWU whether anyone's querying it or not. You're paying for compute by the hour, not by the answer. The serverless SQL pool flips that. It bills you per terabyte scanned, so one badly written query against unpartitioned Parquet can cost more than the dashboard it feeds. Nobody warns finance that the same report can cost 5 cents or 5 euros depending on how the tables are laid out. Here's the part that gets expensive. Microsoft's roadmap now points every net-new analytics build at Fabric, which reuses the same engine ideas. So a lot of teams standing up fresh Synapse workspaces in 2026 are building on a platform their own vendor is already steering away from. Gartner reported in 2023 that less than half of data and analytics teams deliver real value to their org. An idle dedicated pool and a runaway serverless bill are a quick way to land in that half. So the tool does its job. What goes sideways is buying Azure Synapse Analytics as a checkbox, wiring it up once, and never matching the compute model to how people really query. Turn the pool off when it's idle, partition before you scan, and decide on purpose whether Synapse or Fabric is where your next build belongs.

Day 5 · FriENAustinfabric capacity
Most teams size their Fabric capacity wrong, and the bill proves it.
Nobody tells you this when you buy a Fabric capacity: it bills by the second, and you can switch it off. If your F-SKU runs 24/7 but your team only opens reports during the workday, you're paying for a pile of idle hours every night and weekend. Switching it off off-hours is a real line-item cut, and it takes one setting. The F64 line matters more than the SKU size. At F64 and above, anyone can view your Power BI reports without their own Pro license. Below F64, every single viewer needs Pro. So if 300 people open a dashboard, moving up to F64 can cost you less than 300 Pro seats. That math surprises a lot of finance teams. Before you commit to any SKU, open the Fabric Capacity Metrics app. Fabric smooths short spikes over time, then it throttles and eventually rejects operations once you push past the ceiling. Most teams pick a capacity by guessing and never check whether they're sitting at 30% usage or 130%. Oversizing costs you the most here. A capacity that's too big wastes budget on room you never touch. One that's too small throttles your refreshes right when month-end reporting hits. The metrics app shows you which one you are in about ten minutes. So right-sizing a Fabric capacity isn't a guess. It's switching off idle hours, checking the F64 license math, and reading your own usage numbers before you sign off on a SKU.

Day 6 · SatENAustinfabric data warehouse
Most teams pick the Fabric data warehouse for the wrong reason.
They hear "warehouse," picture the old SQL Server box, and expect to tune it the same way. Then they go looking for the index-tuning knob and there isn't one. The Fabric data warehouse manages statistics and data distribution itself. Here's the part people miss. In Fabric you've got two things that both speak SQL: the Warehouse and the Lakehouse SQL endpoint. That endpoint is read-only. If your team needs INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE and real multi-table transactions, you want the Warehouse with full T-SQL. Pick the endpoint by mistake and you're rewriting the whole thing later. Both write Delta Parquet into OneLake, so the storage sitting underneath is identical. What splits them is who's doing the transformation. Engineers who live in Spark notebooks lean Lakehouse. Analysts who think in T-SQL and stored procedures get more done in the Warehouse. And here's the money part nobody plans for: it all runs on one shared capacity. One analyst's runaway CROSS JOIN eats the same compute your scheduled refreshes need. With Microsoft Fabric past 28,000 customers, a lot of teams are meeting that surprise in production. So don't start with Warehouse or Lakehouse. Start with who writes your transformations and what they're fluent in. Answer that and the Fabric data warehouse call gets a lot easier.

Day 9 · TueENAustinincremental refresh power bi
Most teams set up incremental refresh in Power BI the wrong way.
Here's what I see constantly. Your dataset reloads all 60 million rows every single morning, the refresh crawls past 40 minutes, and some days it just times out on shared capacity. Then nobody trusts the number on the dashboard because they can't tell if it's today's figure or last Tuesday's. Incremental refresh fixes this by only reloading the data that changed. You set two parameters, RangeStart and RangeEnd, filter your query on a date column, and Power BI splits the table into partitions. Refresh the last 3 days, keep the last 5 years archived, and leave the old partitions untouched. Most people stop there and still reload the whole recent window anyway. Turn on Detect Data Changes and point it at a last-modified timestamp column. Now even that 3-day window only pulls the rows that actually moved. This isn't a nice-to-have. 67% of organizations say they don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Drexel LeBow and Precisely, 2025). A refresh that times out at 2am and tells no one is exactly how you land in that 67%. Incremental refresh in Power BI is worth doing for one plain reason: a dashboard people can open at 8am and believe.

Day 27 · SatENAustinrow level security power bi
Most teams set up row level security in Power BI and assume the data's locked down. Plenty of people can still see every row.
Here's what row level security really does. You write a DAX filter on a role, assign people to it, and they only see the rows that match. Region managers see their region, and nothing else. Clean. Now the part nobody tests. Anyone with Admin, Member, or Contributor access on the workspace skips RLS completely. So does anyone with edit rights on the semantic model. They see all of it. The filter only kicks in for Viewers you've assigned to a role. Second trap: RLS filters rows, not pages or visuals. Turn on bidirectional cross-filtering to make a slicer behave, and rows can slip across a relationship the role was supposed to block. The report looks fine. The data isn't contained. This is why governance beats a good demo. 67% of organizations say they don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Drexel LeBow + Precisely, 2025). A finance lead seeing a business unit's numbers they were never cleared for is a fast way to land on that list. Row level security in Power BI is only as strong as the access model around it. Get the roles, the workspace permissions, and the cross-filter directions right together, or the whole setup only looks locked down.

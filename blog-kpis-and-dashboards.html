KPIs and Dashboards: Why Yours Show Numbers Nobody Trusts

Quick answer. KPIs and dashboards are the numbers your team tracks and the screen that shows them. In Power BI, a good dashboard pulls five to nine numbers from one clean data model, defines each number as a single DAX measure, and refreshes on its own, so everyone in the room reads the same figure. --- Most teams get KPIs and dashboards wrong, and it shows up in the first leadership review. Two managers open the same tile, see two different revenue numbers, and from that second on, nobody talks about the number anymore. They talk about which number is right. That argument is expensive, and it happens because the screen was built on shaky ground.

This is a plain-English guide to fixing that. You don't need to be a data engineer to follow it. You do need to know where the numbers come from and why they drift.

What are KPIs and dashboards, really?

A KPI is a number you watch because it drives a decision. Revenue this month. Cash in the bank. Customers who left. A dashboard is the single screen that shows those numbers together, refreshed and in one place.

The trap is treating the dashboard as a picture. It looks like design work, so people polish the colors and the layout. Underneath, though, the number on each tile is a calculation, and that calculation is where trust is won or lost. A pretty tile with the wrong figure is worse than a plain one with the right figure, because people believe it.

So the question isn't "how do I make this look good." It's "how do I make every number on here provable." That's the job.

Why do your KPIs and dashboards show numbers people don't trust?

There are three common reasons, and all three sit below the surface.

The first is a flat table. A lot of dashboards start life as one big spreadsheet export where revenue, region, product, and date all live in the same wide sheet. The moment you filter it, Power BI counts rows twice, because the same order shows up on several lines. Your totals jump depending on the filter, and people notice.

The second is a missing definition. When "revenue" means gross on one tile and net-of-discount on another, every tile does its own math. This matters more than it looks. About 94% of business spreadsheets used to make decisions contain errors (Poon et al., 2024). A dashboard built straight on top of those sheets inherits every one of them.

The third is trust itself, and it compounds. 67% of organizations don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Precisely and Drexel LeBow, 2025). If your leadership team had to correct the number on the first tile once, they'll doubt the second tile too, even when it's correct. Poor data quality costs organizations $12.9M per year on average (Gartner), and a big share of that's time spent arguing about figures instead of acting on them.

How do you build a dashboard your leadership team will actually believe?

You build the model first and the screen second. The model is a small set of connected tables called a star schema, and it does the heavy lifting.

Here's the plain version. You split your one wide sheet into a fact table and a few dimension tables. The fact table has the events with numbers in them, like each invoice line with a quantity and an amount. The dimension tables carry the labels: customer, product, region, and a calendar of dates. You connect them with one-to-many relationships, so one product points to many invoice lines and one date points to many invoice lines.

Once the tables are connected this way, Power BI filters cleanly from the label down into the numbers, and the double-counting goes away. A shared calendar table is the most important piece here. Without it, a comparison like "same month last year" won't work reliably.

You do the cleanup in Power Query, you draw the relationships in the model view, and only then do you write the numbers. Skipping to the pretty screen before the model is done is why so many dashboards drift.

Which numbers belong on a dashboard, and which don't?

Fewer than you'd guess. A leadership reads five to nine numbers per view before attention drops off. Pick the ones that trigger a decision and push the rest to a detail page.

Write every number you keep as a measure in DAX, never a column. A measure calculates at the moment it's shown and adjusts to whatever filter is on the page. You'd write revenue as something like `Revenue = SUM(Facts[Amount])`, and margin as its own measure built on top of it. That gives you exactly one definition per number, stored in the model where anyone can open it.

Two Power BI features earn their keep here. The KPI visual Power BI gives you out of the box shows a number against a target and a trend line, which is perfect for a headline figure like revenue versus plan. And the Power BI CALCULATE function lets you change the filter behind a number on purpose, so "revenue, last year, this region" is one clear measure instead of a manual filter someone forgets to set.

The payoff is simple. When someone asks how you got a number, you open the measure and show the formula. The number is checkable, and that's what closes the trust gap a flat sheet leaves open.

How do you keep KPIs and dashboards current and safe?

A dashboard that was right yesterday is worthless today if nobody refreshes it. In Power BI you schedule the refresh: up to eight times a day on a Pro license, and far more often on capacity.

For large fact tables, turn on incremental refresh. Using the RangeStart and RangeEnd parameters, Power BI loads only the new rows instead of pulling the whole history every time. That takes a refresh from hours down to minutes.

So the wrong people don't see the wrong rows, set up Row-Level Security. A regional lead then sees only her region. Before you publish, test it with the View As role feature, so no number reaches the wrong inbox. And keep your environments apart with deployment pipelines, which move changes from development to test to production without touching the live version. You get to try a new number before leadership ever sees it.

How do you build your first trustworthy dashboard, step by step?

Six steps you can start today:

  1. Split your one wide sheet into a fact table and dimension tables for customer, product, region, and date.
  2. Build a dedicated calendar table and connect it to the facts with one-to-many relationships.
  3. Add your cleanup steps in Power Query, so the raw data fixes itself on every refresh.
  4. Write each number as a DAX measure with one documented definition. In Power BI create measure entries rather than calculated columns, so the math follows every filter.
  5. Choose nine numbers at most for the main view, then switch on incremental refresh and Row-Level Security.
  6. Check with View As, and publish through a deployment pipeline only after it passes.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a KPI and a dashboard? A KPI is a single number that drives a decision, like monthly revenue. A dashboard is the screen that brings several KPIs onto one view, kept current on a schedule. You need good KPIs first, then a dashboard to display them.

Do I really need a star schema, or is one table fine? For two or three numbers, one table is fine. Once you filter by region, product, and time, you need a star schema, or the same order gets counted on several lines and your totals jump.

How many numbers should a dashboard show? Five to nine on the main view. After that, attention drops and people stop reading. Keep the deciders up front and move the detail to a second page.

What's a measure, and why can't I just use a column? A measure calculates when it's shown and adjusts to the current filter, so one definition works everywhere on the page. A column is fixed at load time, which is why the same "revenue" can end up meaning two things.

How often should a dashboard refresh? It depends on the decision. A management report is often fine daily, while a sales floor might want hourly. A Pro license allows up to eight scheduled refreshes a day, and capacity allows more.

Can I reuse my existing Excel dashboard? Keep the numbers and the logic, drop the flat structure. Rebuild the sheet into a star schema so you don't carry the old file's errors into the new one.

### [0:40–1:05 lead-in] and [1:05 chapter] Beat 1 — "What can Power BI Copilot actually do?"

[MODALITY: screen-record, live Power BI with the Copilot panel open. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Copilot = a question box on top of your model"]

Spoken (CLAIM): Here is the honest description. Power BI Copilot is a question box that sits on top of the data model you already built.

[MODALITY: screen-record continues, showing each action as it is named]

Spoken (PROOF): You type a question in plain English, like show me revenue by region for the last two quarters, and Copilot writes the query for you, builds a chart, and puts a short written summary underneath it. It can also draft a whole report page from a table, explain what a measure is doing, and suggest visuals you did not think to add. That is real, and on a clean model it is fast.

[MODALITY: on-screen text, kinetic. ON-SCREEN CARD: "It reads your model. It does not fix your model."]

Spoken (PAYOFF): So the rule to take from this beat is simple. Copilot reads the model you give it. It does not correct a messy one. Whatever your data already says, Copilot will say it back faster and with more confidence.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): Which is why the same tool can help one team and embarrass another. Let me show you how that happens.

### [1:05–2:35] Beat 2 — "Why does Copilot give two people two different answers?"

[MODALITY: custom animation. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Two reports. Two revenue numbers."]

Spoken (CLAIM): Most teams already have the same figure defined two or three different ways across their reports. Copilot inherits every one of those definitions.

[MODALITY: animation shows two report tiles, both labelled Revenue, showing different totals, and Copilot pulling from each]

Spoken (PROOF): Say one report counts revenue when the order is placed and another counts it when the invoice is paid. Both are labelled revenue. A person can eyeball that and know which one finance means. Copilot cannot. When your head of sales asks Copilot for last month's revenue, it grabs whichever measure it finds first, and your head of finance asks the same question and gets a different total. Now you have two senior people quoting two numbers in the same meeting, and both of them think the AI confirmed it.

[MODALITY: talking head. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Copilot repeats your definitions, it does not agree them"]

Spoken (PAYOFF): So the takeaway you can use this afternoon is this. Before Copilot, disagreeing numbers were a slow annoyance you sorted out in email. After Copilot, they come out instantly, with a confident summary attached, in front of the people you least want to confuse.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): That tells you where the real work is. It is underneath the question box. So what does Copilot actually need from your data first? That is next.

### [2:35–4:05] Beat 3 — "What does Copilot need from your data first?"

[MODALITY: custom animation, star schema assembling. ON-SCREEN CARD: "One model. Clear names. Agreed measures."]

Spoken (CLAIM): Copilot needs three plain things to give good answers. One data model, human-readable names, and measures that are defined once.

[MODALITY: screen-record showing a tidy field list with plain names, then an animation of one fact table with dimensions connecting]

Spoken (PROOF): First, one model behind the report, so Copilot is reading from a single well-built structure and not stitching together three that half-overlap. Second, names a normal person would type. If your revenue column is called fct_amt_net_2, Copilot will struggle to match a question to it, and so will your team. Third, the measures that matter, revenue, margin, active customers, defined one time in the model so there is exactly one answer for each. When those three things are in place, the question box works the way the demo promised.

[MODALITY: on-screen text. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Copilot is the tap. Your model is the plumbing."]

Spoken (PAYOFF): Here is the takeaway. The demo you saw from Microsoft was running on a model built exactly for the demo. The gap between that and your result is the state of your own model. Close that gap and Copilot is worth the licence. Skip it and you are paying for faster wrong answers.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): Which raises the obvious question. How do you actually tell whether your data is ready? There is a number on this, and it is sobering.

### [4:05–5:40] Beat 4 — "Is your data ready for AI, and how would you know?"

[MODALITY: data-viz, a single animated figure counting up to 12%. ON-SCREEN CARD: "12% say their data is good enough for AI"]

Spoken (CLAIM): Almost every company wants AI on their reports. Very few have the data underneath it in the right shape.

[MODALITY: talking head over the figure]

Spoken (PROOF): Only about 12 percent of organisations say their data is in good enough shape, both quality and access, to actually use for AI. That is from Drexel LeBow and Precisely in 2025. Look at that for a second. Nearly nine in ten teams switching on AI features are switching them on over data they themselves do not fully trust. Copilot does not change that math. It sits right on top of it.

[MODALITY: screen-record, a quick readiness check. ON-SCREEN CARD: "3 questions to ask before Copilot"]

Spoken (PROOF, continued): You can self-check in about a minute. One, if two reports show the same figure, do they always match? Two, could a new analyst open your model and understand the field names without a call? Three, does every key measure have one agreed definition that finance and sales both signed off? If any answer is no, Copilot will amplify that gap in front of whoever is asking.

[MODALITY: on-screen text. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Readiness is a data question, not an AI question"]

Spoken (PAYOFF): So the takeaway is that Copilot readiness is a data question that happens to wear an AI label. The work is boring and it is the whole game. Clean model, clear names, agreed measures.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): And that word agreed is doing a lot of lifting. Let me show you the single biggest one, the one-number problem, and how teams actually solve it.

### [5:40–7:15] Beat 5 — "What is the one-number problem, and how do you fix it?"

[MODALITY: custom animation, many scattered definitions merging into one labelled measure. ON-SCREEN CARD: "One agreed number"]

Spoken (CLAIM): The fix that makes Copilot trustworthy has nothing to do with clever prompting. It comes from agreeing on one definition for each number that matters, and building it into the model once.

[MODALITY: screen-record, defining a single measure in the semantic model, then two reports both pointing at it]

Spoken (PROOF): Here is what that looks like in practice. You sit finance, sales, and operations in one room and you settle the definitions. What counts as an active customer. When revenue is recognised. How margin is calculated. Then that agreed definition gets written into the semantic model as the one measure everyone draws from. Every report points at it. And now when someone asks Copilot for active customers, there is only one possible answer, because there is only one definition in the model for it to reach.

[MODALITY: talking head. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Agree it once. Build it once. Everyone asks freely."]

Spoken (PAYOFF): The takeaway you can act on. The value of Copilot is capped by how many of your core numbers are agreed and built once. Every measure you pin down is a question your whole team can now ask the AI safely. Every measure you leave loose is a wrong answer waiting to be quoted.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): Now, remember the thing I said most teams check last? This is it, and it is the part that keeps people up at night once they see it.

### [7:15–8:50] Beat 6 — "Who gets to see what once Copilot can read everything?"

[MODALITY: custom animation, row-level security, same report, two users, different rows visible. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Copilot can reach any number a user is allowed to reach"]

Spoken (CLAIM): Here is the one most teams check last. Copilot will happily surface any figure a person is allowed to reach. So if your access rights are loose, Copilot becomes the fastest way to find that out.

[MODALITY: screen-record showing row-level security roles, then a Copilot query returning only the rows a user is entitled to]

Spoken (PROOF): Before Copilot, a salesperson would need to know exactly which report tab and which slicer to reach another region's margin. Loose access rights were a risk that mostly stayed asleep because nobody went looking. Copilot wakes it up. Someone types what is our margin by region, and if row-level security is not set correctly, Copilot returns the whole picture, including the parts that person was never meant to see. Copilot is behaving exactly as told. It follows the access rules you gave it, and those rules were too open.

[MODALITY: on-screen text. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Fix who can see what before you widen who can ask"]

Spoken (PAYOFF): So the takeaway, and this is the open loop I promised. Before you turn Copilot on for the whole company, check that row-level security is correct on every model it can reach. Copilot widens who can ask a question. Your access rules decide who should get an answer. Line those up first.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): Put all of this together and you get a short checklist for the week before you switch it on. Here it is.

### [8:50–10:05] Beat 7 — "What should you do before you turn Copilot on?"

[MODALITY: kinetic type, numbered list building one item at a time. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Before you turn Copilot on"]

Spoken (CLAIM): Five checks. Run these before Copilot goes wide, and you skip almost every embarrassing moment I have described.

[MODALITY: on-screen numbered list, each line appears as spoken]

Spoken (PROOF): One. Get to one model per subject area, so Copilot reads a single clean structure. Two. Rename your fields to words a normal person would type. Three. Agree every core measure once, with finance and sales in the room, and build it into the model. Four. Set and test row-level security on every model Copilot can reach. Five. Turn Copilot on for one small, ready team first, watch what it answers for a month, then widen it.

[MODALITY: talking head. ON-SCREEN CARD: "Ready data first. AI second."]

Spoken (PAYOFF): Do those five and Copilot earns its keep, because it is answering from data you already trust. Every one of them is data work you should want done anyway, Copilot or no Copilot. The AI just raises the cost of skipping it.

Spoken (RE-HOOK): So the last question is the one you actually came here to settle. Is it worth it for you, right now?

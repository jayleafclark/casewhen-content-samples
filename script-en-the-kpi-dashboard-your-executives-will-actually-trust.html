### [0:45-2:10] BEAT 1 — What carries over when you rebuild a dashboard?

[MODALITY: custom animation — a dashboard rebuild, three columns lighting up: Sources, Questions, Layout] [ON-SCREEN CARD: "Chapter 2 — What carries over?"]

CLAIM. When you rebuild a KPI dashboard, three things move over almost for free. Your raw source data, the business questions you are trying to answer, and the visual layout people already know.

[MODALITY SHIFT: screen-record — a source table, then a wireframe of the old report]

PROOF. Think about a revenue dashboard. The sales orders sitting in your finance database do not change because you moved to a new report. The questions your CFO asks, revenue by region, margin by product, cash against forecast, those are the same questions she asked last year. And the shape of the report, the cards across the top, the trend line, the regional breakdown, you can copy that layout straight across. Your people already know where to look.

[MODALITY: talking head] [ON-SCREEN TAKEAWAY: "1. Sources, questions, and layout transfer. Do not rebuild them."]

PAYOFF. So when someone quotes you a big number to rebuild a dashboard, push back on the parts that transfer. You are not paying to reinvent the layout or re-source the data. Most of what the users see can carry over.

RE-HOOK. But there is one part that never transfers cleanly, and it is the exact part that decides whether the new number matches the old one. That is next.

### [2:10-3:40] BEAT 2 — What do you always have to rebuild by hand?

[MODALITY: custom animation — the semantic model: a fact table in the center, dimension tables snapping in with one-to-many arrows] [ON-SCREEN CARD: "Chapter 3 — What you rebuild"]

CLAIM. The one thing you always rebuild from scratch is the layer that turns raw data into the numbers, the semantic model. That means the relationships between your tables and the exact definition of every measure.

[MODALITY SHIFT: screen-record — two measure definitions side by side, one small difference marked]

PROOF. Here is where trust goes wrong. "Revenue" sounds like one thing. In the old report it might be net of returns and booked on invoice date. In the rebuild, someone writes the measure gross, or books it on order date, because that seemed right on the day. Now the new dashboard says four point two million and the old one said three point nine. Same source data, two different definitions, two different numbers. Nobody lied. The measure was just rebuilt slightly differently, and no one wrote down which version was correct.

[MODALITY: data-viz — the same bar, two heights, one labelled "gross / order date", one "net / invoice date"] [ON-SCREEN TAKEAWAY: "2. The measure definition is the product. Agree it before you build it."]

PAYOFF. So the real deliverable in a rebuild is the agreed definition of each KPI, written down and put in one place that both people and Copilot read from. The charts are the easy part. Get the definition wrong and the prettiest dashboard on earth will lose the room.

RE-HOOK. Which brings us to the moment every finance leader dreads. Two reports open on the same screen, two revenue numbers, and a very quiet meeting.

### [3:40-5:15] BEAT 3 — Why do two reports show two different revenue numbers?

[MODALITY: talking head] [ON-SCREEN CARD: "Chapter 4 — Two numbers, one truth"]

CLAIM. When two dashboards disagree, it is almost never the database. It is that each report calculates the number its own way, in its own hidden logic.

[MODALITY SHIFT: screen-record — a Power BI report, then a second report, both filtering the same table differently]

PROOF. Picture two dashboards built by two different people. One filters out intercompany sales, the other leaves them in. One counts a deal when it is signed, the other when it is invoiced. Both pull from the same finance table. Both are internally correct. And they will never agree, because the rule that makes the number lives inside each report, not in one shared place. This is exactly why nearly forty percent of CFOs say they do not completely trust their own finance data, according to BlackLine's 2024 survey. It is rarely bad data. It is the same data, counted two ways.

[MODALITY: custom animation — many reports merging into one shared semantic model, a single arrow feeding all of them] [ON-SCREEN TAKEAWAY: "3. One shared model feeds every report. Define once, use everywhere."]

PAYOFF. The fix is to move that rule out of the individual reports and into one shared semantic model that every dashboard reads from. Define revenue once. Then every report, every export, and every Copilot answer returns the same figure, because they are all asking the same place.

RE-HOOK. But that only works if you can find where the definition currently lives. And in most companies, that is the scary part.

### [5:15-6:45] BEAT 4 — Where does a KPI's definition actually live?

[MODALITY: b-roll — a single analyst at a desk, then an empty desk] [ON-SCREEN CARD: "Chapter 5 — Where the definition hides"]

CLAIM. In most companies, the real definition of a KPI is not written anywhere. It lives in one analyst's head and in a filter buried three clicks deep in a report nobody opens.

[MODALITY SHIFT: screen-record — clicking into a report's filter pane, revealing a hidden exclusion]

PROOF. Here is a scene I see over and over. The "active customers" number on the executive dashboard has a rule attached that says exclude any account with no order in the last ninety days. That rule was added two years ago by an analyst who has since left. Nobody wrote it down. It sits inside one report as a filter setting three clicks deep. When that analyst walked out the door, the reasoning behind your headline number walked out too. Now no one can say why active customers dropped, or whether the rule still makes sense.

[MODALITY: talking head] [ON-SCREEN TAKEAWAY: "4. If a definition lives in one person's head, you do not own it. Write it down."]

PAYOFF. So before you rebuild anything, go find where each KPI is really defined and write it into one document your team controls. A KPI you cannot explain in a sentence is a KPI your executives are right to distrust.

RE-HOOK. And there is a second reason two people stare at the same report and see different totals. This one is not about definitions at all.

### [6:45-8:20] BEAT 5 — Why does the same report show different totals to different people?

[MODALITY: custom animation — row-level security: the same report, two viewers, rows filtering in and out per identity] [ON-SCREEN CARD: "Chapter 6 — Same report, different totals"]

CLAIM. Two things make the same dashboard show different numbers to different people. Who is allowed to see which rows, and when the data last refreshed.

[MODALITY SHIFT: screen-record — row-level security roles in Power BI, one region manager view, one CFO view]

PROOF. Take row-level security first. Your regional managers should only see their own region, so the report filters rows by who is looking. That is correct behaviour. But it means when a manager quotes a total in a meeting and the CFO sees a bigger one, they are both right, and it looks like the dashboard is wrong. Now add refresh timing. If your report updates at six in the morning and someone opens it at four in the afternoon after a big shipment, the number on screen is eight hours old. The report did exactly what it was told, it simply has not run again since this morning. Both of these read as "the dashboard cannot be trusted" when really the access rules and the refresh clock were never explained.

[MODALITY: data-viz — a small "Data as of 06:00" timestamp appearing on a report card] [ON-SCREEN TAKEAWAY: "5. Put the access scope and the refresh time on the report itself."]

PAYOFF. The cure is boring and it works. Show the last refresh time on every page, in plain sight. And tell each executive, once, what their view is scoped to. When people understand why their number differs, the difference stops reading as an error.

RE-HOOK. So you have agreed definitions, one shared model, and clear access rules. Now comes the step almost everyone skips, the one that decides whether the new dashboard survives its first week.

### [8:20-10:20] BEAT 6 — How do you switch over without losing the room?

[MODALITY: talking head, slower, this is the core line] [ON-SCREEN CARD: "Chapter 7 — The switch"]

CLAIM. Here is the step most teams skip. Before you turn off the old report, you run the new one next to it and reconcile the numbers, line by line, until they match or you can explain every gap.

[MODALITY SHIFT: screen-record — old report and new report side by side, a reconciliation sheet, differences being ticked off]

PROOF. This is the open loop I promised you at the start, and it is the whole game. Most teams build the new dashboard, announce it, and switch off the old one on the same day. Then in week one an executive finds one number that differs from what they remember, and the trust is gone. It is very hard to earn back. So do the opposite. Keep both reports live for two or three closes. Put the old number and the new number side by side for every KPI. Where they match, you have proof. Where they differ, you either fix the new measure or you write down exactly why the new one is more correct. Then you walk the CFO through that sheet before you switch. When she has seen the new number tie out to the number she already trusted, she owns it. That is the moment the dashboard becomes hers instead of yours.

[MODALITY: custom animation — old and new lines converging, then the old one fading out] [ON-SCREEN TAKEAWAY: "6. Never cut over cold. Run parallel, reconcile, then retire the old report."]

PAYOFF. A parallel run of two or three cycles costs you a few weeks. A rushed cutover that loses the leadership team costs you the whole project, because a dashboard they stopped trusting is one you will be rebuilding anyway.

RE-HOOK. So let me pull the whole thing together into what you can do this week.

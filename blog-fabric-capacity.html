Fabric Capacity: What You Actually Pay to Run It

Quick answer.

Fabric capacity is the shared pool of compute you buy to run everything inside Microsoft Fabric: Power BI, data pipelines, warehouses, lakehouses, and notebooks. You pick a size, called an F SKU, from F2 up to F2048. Every workload draws from that one pool, and Fabric capacity is billed per capacity unit, hourly or reserved.

You picked a Fabric capacity size, and the first bill came in higher than anyone on the team expected. It happens because most teams pick a size by guessing: a big number to feel safe, or a small one to feel cheap. Neither guess matches how the platform actually charges you. This guide covers how Fabric capacity works, what it costs, and how to size it so you stop paying for compute nobody uses.

Microsoft Fabric passed 28,000 customers within its first two years (Microsoft, Ignite 2025). A lot of those buyers signed a capacity before anyone worked out what they needed. You can do better with about ten minutes of math.

What is Fabric capacity, in plain terms?

Microsoft Fabric is one product that bundles the tools a data team uses: Power BI for reports, Data Factory for pipelines, a warehouse and a lakehouse for storage and queries, and notebooks for code. Fabric capacity is the compute that runs all of it. You buy one capacity, and every tool shares it.

Two ideas do most of the work here.

The first is the capacity unit, or CU. It's how Fabric measures compute. An F64 capacity gives you 64 CUs to spread across every job you run. Bigger F number, more CUs, higher price.

The second is OneLake. OneLake is the single storage layer under Fabric, and it keeps all your data in one place. Storage is billed on its own, apart from compute. So your Fabric capacity pays for the processing, and OneLake pays for the data sitting at rest. Keeping those two costs separate in your head stops a lot of budget confusion later.

Why does your Fabric capacity bill jump when nothing changed?

Here's a surprise a lot of teams hit. Your reports run fine for weeks. Then one Monday everything slows down, and the next invoice is higher. Your data didn't change. What changed is how much compute your jobs asked for.

Fabric smooths compute over time. When a heavy job runs, Fabric lets it borrow compute above your limit for a short spike. That feature is called bursting. Fabric then spreads the cost of that spike over the following hours, up to 24 of them. Small bursts stay invisible on the bill.

The trouble starts when your team keeps running more than the capacity provides. The smoothing runs out. Fabric then throttles you. First, interactive reports wait a few extra seconds. Then longer. Eventually background refreshes get delayed. A refresh that used to finish at 6 a.m. now lands at 9 a.m., right when the board opens the report.

This matters because trust is fragile. Sixty-seven percent of organizations don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Precisely and Drexel LeBow, 2025). A report that's late or throttled feeds that doubt. So a capacity that's slightly too small doesn't just cost speed. It costs confidence in the numbers.

How do you size Fabric capacity without overpaying?

You size it from real usage, not from headcount or a gut number. Here are the steps we run for clients.

  1. Measure your real peak. Install the free Microsoft Fabric Capacity Metrics app. Let it collect two to four weeks of data. Look at your busiest hour, not your average. That peak CU number is what you size against.
  2. Match an F SKU to that peak. Round up to the next size that covers your peak with a little headroom. Use the table below.
  3. Choose pay-as-you-go or reserved. Pay-as-you-go bills per second and can be switched off. A one-year reservation costs about 41 percent less for steady, all-day workloads. Run production on reserved, and keep dev on pay-as-you-go.
  4. Switch off what you don't need overnight. A pay-as-you-go capacity used only for development can be turned off after hours. That stops the meter and cuts the bill for the time nobody's working.
  5. Watch the F64 line. At F64 and above, anyone can view a Power BI report for free. Below F64, every viewer needs their own Power BI Pro license at about $14 per user per month.

When should you use Microsoft Fabric, and when should you wait?

Knowing when to use Microsoft Fabric comes down to two things: what you already run, and how many workloads you want under one roof. Fabric earns its price when you want Power BI, pipelines, and a warehouse sharing one bill and one storage layer, OneLake. That single-platform setup removes the copies of data that pile up across separate tools.

Fabric earns it less when all you need is a handful of Power BI reports for a small team. In that case, Power BI Pro at about $14 per user per month often costs far less than the smallest F2 capacity. There's no rule that says you must move. Plenty of teams run Power BI Pro for years and never need a capacity.

So the honest test is workload, not hype. Count your workloads. One reporting tool for ten people points to Pro. Pipelines, a warehouse, data science, and reporting for the whole company point to Fabric.

What does a Fabric implementation partner actually do here?

A Fabric implementation partner earns its fee in the sizing and the guardrails, long before any dashboard gets built. The value shows up in three places.

First, right-sizing. A partner reads your Capacity Metrics data and picks the SKU that fits your real peak, so you skip both the oversized capacity and the throttled one.

Second, cost control. A partner sets up the pay-as-you-go and reserved split, turns off idle capacities, and puts an alert on CU use so a runaway job gets caught the same day, not on the invoice.

Third, governance. That means row-level security, workspace roles, and clear owners for every semantic model in OneLake, so the reports people read can be trusted.

Sizing Fabric capacity well is a small, repeatable calculation. The steps above get most teams to the right number on their own. A partner is worth it when the workloads are mixed, the data is large, or the cost of a slow month-end close is high enough that guessing isn't an option.

Frequently asked questions

What is the smallest Fabric capacity you can buy? The smallest is F2, with 2 capacity units, at roughly $263 a month on pay-as-you-go. It suits development and small workloads, though report viewers below F64 still need Power BI Pro licenses.

Do I still need Power BI Pro licenses with Fabric capacity? It depends on the size. On F64 and above, report consumers view content for free. Below F64, every viewer needs a Pro license. Report authors and publishers need a Pro license at any size.

Can I turn Fabric capacity off to save money? Yes, on pay-as-you-go. You can switch a capacity off outside work hours and stop the meter, which is handy for development capacities. A reserved capacity bills at a flat rate whether it's running or idle, so you keep it on.

How is Fabric capacity different from Power BI Premium? Fabric F SKUs replace the old Premium per-capacity plan for most buyers. An F64 lines up with the old P1 capacity, including free report viewing, but an F SKU also runs pipelines, warehouses, and notebooks, not just Power BI.

What is a capacity unit? A capacity unit, or CU, is how Fabric measures compute. Your F SKU number is your CU count, so an F16 gives you 16 CUs. Every job you run, from a refresh to a query, draws CUs from that shared pool.

How do I know if my capacity is too small? Open the Fabric Capacity Metrics app and look for throttling. If interactive reports are waiting or refreshes are getting delayed during your busy hours, your peak use is above what the capacity provides, and you should size up.

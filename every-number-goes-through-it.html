Can't Get Data Without Asking IT? How to Give Your Team Self-Serve Access Safely

Quick answer. When your sales and marketing teams can't get data without asking IT, a governed self-serve setup gets you out of the queue. You put the common numbers in one trusted model, open up everyday questions for people to answer on their own, and route only real changes through a short request list. Teams wait less, and the numbers still reconcile at month-end. ---

If your sales and marketing teams can't get data without asking IT or RevOps first, you already know how the week goes. Someone needs pipeline by segment for a Tuesday review. They ping RevOps. RevOps is heads-down on the board pack, so the request sits. By the time the number comes back, the meeting already happened, and the call got made on a screenshot from three weeks ago.

This is the quiet tax on a lot of revenue teams. The people closest to the customer can open the dashboard, but the second they want a cut that isn't already built, they're back in line behind everyone else. You bought the tools and you hired the analysts, and your reps still can't answer a plain question about their own accounts without opening a ticket.

The good part is that this is a setup problem, and setup problems have fixes. You don't have to choose between letting everyone touch the numbers and making everyone wait on one person.

Why does every number go through IT or RevOps?

It comes down to who's allowed to change the numbers everyone trusts. In most companies, the reliable version of revenue, pipeline, and margin lives in one model that only a couple of people can safely edit. That's a reasonable instinct. If ten people can redefine "qualified pipeline," you get ten answers and a fight in every meeting.

So the safe habit became "ask RevOps." Everyone else gets read-only access to a handful of built reports, or nothing at all. The moment a question falls outside those built views, it turns into a request. RevOps becomes the one door every number walks through, and that door has a line.

The other reason is that definitions aren't written down where people can see them. When a rep doesn't know whether "closed" means signed or invoiced, self-serve gives them a wrong answer with full confidence, and now you've got a bigger mess than a slow ticket. So RevOps keeps the keys, the queue grows, and the data bottleneck gets blamed on headcount when the way access was set up is the problem.

What is it costing you when teams can't get data without asking IT?

More than the hours. The cost shows up in the decisions.

Your pipeline reviews turn into guessing sessions on stale numbers, because the fresh cut didn't come back in time. Your quarterly board meeting eats twenty minutes on "whose number is right" instead of what to do about it. And 82% of companies say data silos disrupt their workflows (ZoomInfo), which is a polite way of saying people wait on each other all day.

Trust takes the hit too. When only 40% of executives always trust their own data (Talend), a slow, opaque request process makes it worse. If a number took two weeks and nobody can see how it was built, people go check it against a spreadsheet on the side, and now you're paying to maintain two versions of the truth.

It slows the close, too. Month-end takes about eight days at a typical company, and the strongest finance teams land it in under three (SolveXia). A lot of that gap is people waiting on someone else to pull, reconcile, and confirm a figure. When every number goes through one team, month-end inherits that queue.

And it wastes the reporting you already paid for. Around 70% of the dashboards built inside companies go barely used (RevealBI). Some of that is because the built views never answer the follow-up question, so people give up and file a request instead. A dashboard that can't answer the next question sends everyone right back to the line.

What does good self-serve access look like?

Good doesn't mean everyone can build whatever they want. It means the everyday questions have a safe, fast answer that doesn't need RevOps, and the numbers still agree.

Here's the shape of it. The numbers that matter (revenue, pipeline, margin, whatever your board watches) live in one trusted model with one agreed definition each. On top of that, your teams get self-service analytics for the common questions: slice by region, by rep, by product, by month, without filing anything. When someone needs a brand-new metric or a new data source, that's the only thing that goes into a request queue, and the queue is short because it isn't clogged with "can you filter this by segment."

The difference between the two worlds is easy to see side by side.

How do you open up self-serve without losing control of the numbers?

You govern the model, not the people. Four things make that work, and none of them require you to trust that everyone will be careful.

One trusted model with agreed definitions. Every important number is calculated once, in one place, so "revenue" means the same thing on the sales dashboard and the finance report. This is the piece that makes self-serve safe, because people can slice all they want and still land on the same total.

Row-level security. This controls who sees which rows, so a regional manager only sees their region and a rep only sees their accounts. Once that's in place, you can open access wide without exposing anything you shouldn't, which is usually the fear that keeps everything locked down.

Certified reports and datasets. The numbers your team is allowed to trust get a visible stamp, so people know the difference between an official figure and something a colleague built on the side. That stops the slow drift into a dozen private spreadsheets.

A short request queue for real changes only. New metrics and new data sources still go to your data team, and that's fine. The queue works because it's finally small, since the everyday questions never reach it.

What your team should ask for first

You don't build all of that at once, and you don't need to know how any of it is engineered to ask for the right things. This is the short list to hand your data lead or your consultant, in order.

First, ask for the agreed definitions of your top ten numbers, written down and locked into one model. If your teams don't share definitions, self-serve will hand out wrong answers fast, so this comes before anything else.

Second, ask for row-level security to be turned on, so access can be opened without exposing the wrong rows. Third, ask for one clean, certified self-serve report or app that covers the questions your teams ask most, so most requests never need a person. Fourth, ask what stays in the request queue and roughly how long those take, so "new metric" has a known path instead of vanishing into a backlog.

That's the decision-level version. The engineering underneath (the semantic model, the security roles, the refresh) is the part worth handing to someone who does it every week, because a self-serve setup that hands out a wrong number is worse than a slow ticket.

Frequently asked questions

Isn't self-serve just going to create more conflicting numbers? Only if you skip the model. When every important number is defined once in one trusted model, people can slice and filter all they want and still land on the same total. The conflicting-numbers problem comes from letting everyone define metrics on their own, which governed self-serve is built to prevent.

How is this different from just giving everyone read access to dashboards? Read access lets people look at what's already built. The moment they need a cut that isn't there, they're back to filing a request. Self-serve means they can answer the follow-up question themselves, inside the guardrails you set, so the built views aren't a dead end.

Do we need Microsoft Fabric for this, or is Power BI enough? Most teams get there with Power BI: a well-built model, row-level security, and certified datasets. Fabric matters when your data volume or the number of teams reading the same tables outgrows what Power BI alone handles well. It's a threshold, not a starting requirement.

Won't opening up access create security problems? That's what row-level security handles. It controls who sees which rows, so you can open self-serve widely and still keep a manager from seeing another region's numbers. The access gets broader and the exposure stays narrow.

What does bad data actually cost us while we wait? Gartner puts the average cost of poor data quality at $12.9M a year per organization. A lot of that is decisions made on stale or wrong numbers, which is exactly what happens when the fresh figure sits in a queue past the meeting it was needed for.

How long does it take to set up governed self-serve? A focused first pass (agreed definitions on your core numbers, row-level security, and one certified self-serve report) usually runs a few weeks, not months. The full rollout across every team takes longer, but you feel the relief on the reports that matter well before then.

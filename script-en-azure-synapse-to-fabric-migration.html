Chapter 3. Why won't my Synapse workspace just import into Fabric?

[3:10-3:35] CLAIM Spoken: "Here's the blunt answer. A Synapse workspace and a Fabric workspace are different objects sitting on different back ends. There's no lift-and-shift because there's nothing to lift into. You recreate each item where it lives now." (MODALITY: on-screen text) (ON-SCREEN: card "No workspace import. You recreate item by item." )

[3:35-4:35] DEMO Spoken: "Watch what actually happens. In Synapse, a linked service points at your storage and your integration runtime. In Fabric, storage is OneLake, and that concept is built in, so the linked service doesn't carry over the same way, you re-point it. Your SQL objects, tables, views, procedures, get scripted out and rerun. Your pipelines get rebuilt activity by activity. Even where Microsoft ships a migration helper for warehouse schema, it's giving you generated scripts to review, not a one-click move. So the honest mental model is this. You stand up an empty Fabric workspace next to Synapse, and you carry each item over by rebuilding it, then you validate the numbers match." Chapter 4. Where does my dedicated SQL warehouse go?

[4:55-5:25] CLAIM Spoken: "Your dedicated SQL pool becomes a Fabric Warehouse. Same idea, relational tables and T-SQL, but a different engine underneath, and that difference decides how much of your old setup survives." (MODALITY: on-screen text) (ON-SCREEN: card "Dedicated SQL Pool → Fabric Warehouse (different engine)")

[5:25-6:40] DEMO Spoken: "Here's the part that trips people. In a dedicated SQL pool, you sized compute with DWUs, and you tuned performance by choosing a distribution for every table, hash, round robin, or replicate, plus your own indexes. You owned all of that. Fabric Warehouse takes it away on purpose. Storage is Delta and parquet in OneLake. There's no DWU dial. There's no distribution key for you to pick, the engine handles data layout. So the parts of your DDL that were pure performance tuning, the DISTRIBUTION clauses, the manual index strategy, don't just break, they're gone as a concept. Your table structure carries. Your tuning doesn't. It's a different model. If your team spent years hand-tuning distributions, that knowledge doesn't transfer, and the new engine wants you to trust it instead." (MODALITY: custom animation) (ANIMATION, "engine swap": Left, a dedicated SQL pool drawn as fixed compute nodes with coloured "hash / round robin / replicate" tags on each table, and a DWU slider. On transition, the DWU slider fades out, the distribution tags detach and dissolve, and the tables flow down into a single OneLake layer rendered as Delta/parquet files. The message on screen: "Schema carries. Distribution + index tuning doesn't.") (ON-SCREEN: keyword "Fabric Warehouse" + "OneLake (Delta)" pinned)

[6:40-7:05] PAYOFF + RE-HOOK Spoken: "Takeaway three. Keep your table schemas, drop your distribution and index tuning, and re-baseline performance on the new engine before you promise anyone a speed number. Now, schema is one thing. Your actual T-SQL code is another, and this is the gap I promised you at the start." (MODALITY: talking head) (ON-SCREEN: numbered takeaway "3. Schema carries; re-baseline performance, don't assume it.")

Chapter 5. What T-SQL breaks when I rebuild the warehouse?

[7:05-7:35] CLAIM Spoken: "Fabric Warehouse speaks T-SQL, but a smaller dialect than a dedicated SQL pool. So some code you've run for years won't compile, and you want to find that on day one, not during go-live weekend." (MODALITY: on-screen text) (ON-SCREEN: card "Fabric Warehouse T-SQL = a subset. Audit your procs first.")

[7:35-8:45] DEMO Spoken: "Let me make this concrete. IDENTITY columns, the auto-incrementing keys a lot of warehouses lean on, don't behave the way they did, so any load that relied on them needs rethinking. A handful of data types aren't supported, so a column definition can just reject. Some procedural patterns, certain temp table tricks, a few functions, come back as errors. This is exactly the thing that stalls a go-live. The reports look done, then one nightly stored procedure that builds your finance summary hits an unsupported clause and the whole refresh stops. So the move that saves the project is boring. Before you scope anything, script out every stored procedure and every table definition, and run them against an empty Fabric Warehouse. The red squiggles are your real backlog. That list is your migration, everything else is just moving rows." (MODALITY: screen-record) (SCREEN-RECORD: Fabric Warehouse query editor. Paste a real-looking stored procedure with an IDENTITY column and an unsupported data type. Run it. Two errors surface in the messages pane. Numbers are illustrative, shown as a working method not a claim.) (Visible change: zoom-punch on each error, then cut to the tally)

[8:45-9:05] PAYOFF + RE-HOOK Spoken: "Takeaway four. Run your existing procedures and DDL against Fabric before you quote a date. Chapter 6. Do my pipelines and Spark notebooks survive the move?

[9:05-9:35] CLAIM Spoken: "Split answer, and it matters for your timeline. Your Spark notebooks come across pretty cleanly. Your pipelines mostly don't, you rebuild them." (MODALITY: on-screen text) (ON-SCREEN: card "Notebooks: mostly port. Pipelines: recreate.")

[9:35-10:45] DEMO Spoken: "Start with the good side. Spark is Spark. Your notebook code, the PySpark and the SQL inside it, moves to Fabric notebooks with real reuse. What changes is the plumbing around it. The pool config is different, library management is handled another way, and anywhere your code read from a storage account, it now reads from a lakehouse in OneLake, so those paths get re-pointed. Now the pipelines. A Synapse pipeline looks almost identical to a Data Factory pipeline, same activities, same canvas, and that similarity fools people. Fabric Data Factory pipelines are a separate item, and there's no import, so you rebuild each pipeline activity by activity. Some connectors and the older mapping data flows don't have a one-for-one match, so a few activities need a genuine redesign, not a paste. The simple rule, notebooks are a copy-and-fix, pipelines are a rebuild-and-test." (MODALITY: screen-record) (SCREEN-RECORD: Fabric. First a notebook, paste PySpark from Synapse, it runs, mark the single changed line where an ADLS path becomes a lakehouse path. Then cut to a Fabric Data Factory pipeline canvas and rebuild a two-activity Copy pipeline from scratch, showing there's no "import from Synapse" entry point.) (Visible change: split the segment, notebook half then pipeline half, callout on the re-pointed path)

Chapter 7. What will Fabric capacity actually cost me?

[11:05-11:35] CLAIM Spoken: "This is where the cost surprise shows up. A dedicated SQL pool was compute you rented just for the warehouse. Fabric capacity is one shared pool that your warehouse, your pipelines, your Spark, and your Power BI reports all pull from at the same time." (MODALITY: on-screen text / data-viz) (ON-SCREEN: card "Dedicated compute → one shared Fabric capacity (F-SKU)")

[11:35-12:30] DEMO Spoken: "Here's the trap in one picture. You size a Fabric capacity for your warehouse load, looks fine. Then month-end hits. The pipelines run, the finance team opens twenty Power BI reports, and the Spark job kicks off, all against that same capacity. Fabric smooths short spikes, which sounds helpful, until sustained demand tips it over and it starts throttling. Now reports crawl and the capacity chart still looks healthy, so nobody knows why. Fabric passed twenty-eight thousand customers as of Microsoft's Ignite last year, so a lot of teams are flipping this on right now, and the shared-capacity bill is the single most common thing they didn't plan for. The fix is to size on your peak concurrent load, everything running together, not the warehouse in isolation, and to run Synapse and Fabric side by side long enough to watch the real numbers in the capacity metrics app before you switch off the old Synapse setup."

Data Silos Are Costing You Deals. Here's the Connected-View Fix

Quick answer. Data silos are separate copies of your business data that never talk to each other, so sales, finance, and operations each work from a different version of the truth. That gap shows up as slow quotes, wrong forecasts, and deals that stall while your team argues about whose number is right. ---

Data silos are costing you deals right now, and the frustrating part is you probably can't see it happening. It rarely shows up as one dramatic breakdown. It shows up as a quote that took three days when the customer wanted an answer by Friday, a forecast the board stopped believing, and one account where sales, finance, and operations each pulled a different number. Put together, that's what data silos hurting business actually means. It's a string of small delays and disagreements that add up to lost revenue, spread across tools that were never meant to talk to each other.

You feel it most in the room where decisions get made. A pipeline review turns into twenty minutes on whose spreadsheet is correct. A deal that should have closed sits an extra week because nobody could confirm the price and the delivery date in the same afternoon. By the time your teams agree on the number, the customer already signed with a competitor who answered faster.

This is one of the most common reasons good companies lose deals they should win. And it's fixable without ripping out your tools.

How are data silos hurting business results and deals?

A silo is what you get when each team keeps its own copy of the data and none of those copies agree. Sales has the pipeline in the CRM. Finance has revenue and margin in a set of spreadsheets. Operations has stock and delivery in another tool. Each one is right in its own corner, and none of them line up.

That split slows down the exact moments where deals are won. When a rep needs a price approved, they wait on finance. When finance builds the forecast, they wait on sales to confirm what's real. When the customer asks a simple question, the answer takes a day because it lives in three places.

The numbers back up how widespread this is. 82% of companies say data silos disrupt their workflows (ZoomInfo), which is a polite way of saying people wait on each other all day. And when only 40% of executives always trust their own data (Talend), the instinct is to double-check everything by hand, which slows the business down even more.

The cost is easy to miss because no single deal ever gets tagged "lost to a data silo." You just see a lower close rate and a sales cycle that runs longer than it should.

Why do data silos cost you deals?

It comes down to speed and trust, and silos hurt both.

Speed first. Every question that crosses a team boundary turns into a wait. A rep can't quote until finance confirms the margin. Finance can't forecast until sales cleans up the pipeline. Operations can't promise a date until someone checks the real stock. Each handoff adds a day, and a customer deciding between you and a competitor doesn't wait for your internal process to catch up.

Then trust. When two reports disagree on the same number, people stop believing both of them. So they go build a private spreadsheet on the side to feel sure, and now you're paying to maintain yet another version of the truth. Poor upstream data quality makes it worse: 94% of business spreadsheets used in decision-making contain errors (Poon et al., 2024), so the side copies people trust the most are often the ones that are wrong.

It shows up at the close, too. Month-end takes about eight days at a typical company, and the strongest finance teams land it in under three (SolveXia). A lot of that gap is people waiting to pull, reconcile, and confirm figures that never sat in one place to begin with. A slow close means you're planning the next quarter on numbers that are already three weeks old.

And it wastes what you already bought. Around 70% of the dashboards built inside companies go barely used (RevealBI), often because each one pulls from a different silo and none of them tell the same story. Gartner puts the average cost of poor data quality at $12.9M a year per organization, and a big share of that is decisions made on the wrong number while the right one sat in another tool.

What does a connected view actually look like?

Good doesn't mean one giant tool that runs everything. It means your core numbers live in one place that every team reads from, so revenue means the same thing on the sales dashboard and the finance report. People call this a single source of truth, and it's the practical fix for silos.

Here's the shape of it. The numbers that matter to the business (revenue, pipeline, margin, stock, whatever your deals depend on) get one agreed definition each and sit in one connected data view. Sales, finance, and operations all read from that same view instead of their own copies. When a rep quotes, the price is already there. When the board asks for the forecast, there's one number everyone can trace back to its source.

What should you fix first?

You don't fix this by buying more software, and you don't need to know how any of it is built to ask for the right things. Start at the decision level, in this order.

First, name the pain in real deals. Pull five recent deals that slipped or stalled and trace where the wait happened. You'll usually find the same two or three gaps show up again and again, and that tells your team exactly which silos to connect first.

Second, ask for one agreed definition of your top numbers, written down. If your teams don't share definitions, connecting the tools just spreads the disagreement faster. Revenue, margin, and pipeline should each mean one thing across the company.

Third, ask for one connected view of those numbers that all your teams pull from, instead of each team keeping its own copy. This is the single source of truth, and it's the piece that makes the quotes fast and the forecast believable.

That's the level you decide at. The build underneath is worth handing to people who do it every week, because a connected view that hands out a wrong number is worse than a slow one.

### What your team actually does about it

One level down, so you know what you're paying for. Your data team maps the tools the numbers come from, agrees the definitions with finance and sales, then builds one model that calculates each number once and serves it to every report. Row-level security controls who sees which rows, so a regional manager sees only their region while everyone still reads from the same model. Once that's in place, the dashboards stop disagreeing because they're all pulling from the same source. That's the whole job, and it's a few weeks of focused work for the core numbers, not a year-long rebuild.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly is a data silo in a business? A data silo is a set of business data that lives in one team's tool or spreadsheets and doesn't connect to the rest. Sales data in the CRM, finance data in Excel, and operations data in a separate tool are three silos. Each is fine on its own. The problem is they don't agree, so no one number covers the whole business.

How do I know if data silos are costing us deals? Watch for slow quotes, forecasts the board doesn't believe, and meetings that stall on whose number is right. If your reps regularly wait on another team to answer a customer, or two reports show different revenue for the same period, silos are already costing you deals. The five-slipped-deals exercise above makes it concrete fast.

Do we need to replace all our tools to fix this? No. You keep the tools your teams already use. The fix is a connected view that reads from them and calculates each number once, so people stop keeping their own copies. Replacing tools is expensive and rarely the actual problem. The problem is that the tools you have never talk to each other.

How long does it take to get a connected view? A focused first pass on your core numbers usually runs a few weeks, not months. You agree the definitions, connect the main tools, and stand up one trusted view. The full rollout across every team takes longer, but you feel the deals moving faster on the numbers that matter well before then.

Isn't this just an IT problem? No, and treating it as one is why it never gets fixed. The definitions of revenue, margin, and pipeline are business decisions that only finance and sales can settle. IT can connect the tools, but if the business hasn't agreed what the numbers mean, connecting them just spreads the disagreement. This starts as a leadership call.

What does bad data cost us while we wait? Gartner estimates poor data quality costs the average organization $12.9M a year. Most of it comes from choices made on stale or wrong numbers, which is exactly what happens when the right figure sits in another team's tool past the moment you needed it.

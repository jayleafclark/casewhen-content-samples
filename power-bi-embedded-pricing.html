Power BI Embedded Pricing Compared: What You're Actually Paying For

Quick answer. Power BI embedded pricing is capacity pricing rather than per-user pricing. You rent Azure compute by the hour so people outside your company can open reports inside your app without a Power BI login. It starts near $735 a month for the smallest capacity and scales up from there. You can switch it off to cut the bill.

You're the one who has to approve Power BI Embedded pricing for a customer-facing product, and the quote came back as one lump monthly figure with no per-user line on it, so you can't tell what you're actually paying for. What you're looking at is capacity pricing. You rent a fixed amount of Azure compute by the hour, and that one capacity serves reports to every customer who opens your app, from a handful to many thousands, none of them needing a Power BI login of their own.

You're probably reading this because someone wants to show live dashboards to your customers, and the quote you got doesn't match the $14-a-seat number you had in your head. That's normal. Embedded runs on its own model, built for showing reports to people who will never sign in to Power BI themselves, like the users of your product or the clients in a portal you run.

Get this wrong and it shows up in two places. You either over-buy a big capacity that sits mostly idle and burns thousands a month, or you under-buy, the reports crawl at peak hours, and your customers feel it. Neither of those is a licensing footnote. Over-buying is wasted cash, and under-buying is a product that looks slow to the people paying you.

What's Power BI Embedded pricing, in plain numbers?

Embedded is sold as Azure capacity, called A SKUs. You pay for a fixed amount of compute, billed by the hour, that serves reports to everyone who opens your app, however many people that turns out to be.

The smallest tier, A1, runs about $735 a month if you leave it on around the clock. Each step up roughly doubles the size and the price. A4 lands near $5,900 a month, and the tiers keep climbing from there. The right size depends on how many people hit your reports at the same time and how heavy those reports are, not on your total user count.

Here's the lever most buyers miss. You can switch an A SKU off. It bills by the hour, so if your customers only look at reports during business hours, you can shut the capacity down nights and weekends and pay for a fraction of the month. A capacity that would cost $735 running non-stop can cost far less if you run it 50 hours a week instead of 168. That single setting is often the difference between a sensible bill and a wasteful one.

How is Power BI Embedded different from Power BI Pro cost?

Power BI Pro cost is simple: $14 per user a month, and everyone who opens a report needs a seat. That model works great inside your company, where you can count the people and hand each one a login. It falls apart the moment you want to show reports to hundreds or thousands of outside customers, because buying each of them a $14 seat is off the table.

That's the split. The Power BI Pro price of $14 a user a month is a per-person deal for your own staff. Embedded is a per-capacity deal for outside viewers who never log in. So the question that matters is simple: who's looking at the report? Internal team that shares dashboards, you pay per seat. Your customers viewing dashboards inside your product, you pay for capacity.

A quick gut check. If you have 20 internal people who need reports, per-user seats cost about $280 a month and you're done. If you have 5,000 customers who each glance at a dashboard now and then, seats are a non-starter and a single Embedded capacity carries all of them for a fixed monthly number.

Two notes a buyer should take from that table. First, the per-user tiers and the capacity tiers answer different questions, so comparing $14 to $735 head to head is the wrong sum. Second, Microsoft has folded a lot of this into Fabric capacities, and an F64 or larger can serve internal viewers without giving each one a paid seat. If your project is both internal reporting at scale and customer-facing embedding, a Fabric capacity can sometimes cover both, and it's worth asking your vendor to price that path too.

When does Power BI Embedded actually make sense?

Embedded earns its cost in one clear case: you're putting Power BI reports in front of people who aren't your employees and shouldn't have to log in to Microsoft. Customers inside your software. Clients in a portal. Partners looking at a shared scorecard. For that, capacity pricing is the sane choice and the only one that scales.

If the reports stay inside your company, you almost certainly don't need Embedded. Per-user Pro seats, or a Fabric capacity if the headcount is large, will cost less and give you fewer moving parts. Buying an Embedded SKU to serve your own staff is a common way to overspend.

The decision is a two-line conversation you can have without opening a report. Who sees the dashboards, insiders or outsiders. And how many are looking at once. Those two answers point straight at either seats or capacity, and they tell you which capacity size to start with.

What makes the bill bigger than the capacity SKU?

The SKU is the part you can read off a price sheet. The expensive part is the data feeding it. If the numbers in your embedded reports are wrong or slow, you have paid for capacity and shipped your customers something they won't trust, which is worse than shipping nothing.

The figures on this are blunt. Gartner puts the average cost of poor data quality at $12.9M a year per organization. And 67% of companies say they don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Drexel LeBow and Precisely, 2025). On top of that, roughly 70% of the dashboards teams build go unused (RevealBI), usually because people don't believe the numbers. Embed a report your own customers don't trust and you get the same result in public, in front of the people you're trying to keep.

So the money that matters sits in the modeling work underneath, well past the jump from an A1 to an A4: one clean data source, one agreed definition per number, refreshes that finish on time. Skip that and you're paying a monthly capacity bill to broadcast numbers nobody relies on.

How do you keep Power BI Embedded pricing under control?

You keep it in check with four decisions, and none of them need you to build a report.

  1. Size to peak, not to total users. Pick the capacity that handles the most people looking at once, not your whole customer list. Start one tier down from what feels safe and watch it before you scale up.
  2. Switch it off when nobody is looking. If your customers view reports during the day, shut the capacity down at night and on weekends. This is the single biggest saving on the whole bill.
  3. Separate insiders from outsiders. Serve staff with per-user seats or a Fabric capacity and reserve Embedded for the outside viewers who need it. Don't pay capacity rates to show reports to your own team.
  4. Fix the data before you scale the SKU. A bigger capacity makes a slow, wrong report faster and wronger. Sort the model out first, then size the compute.

Here's the one part that goes a level deeper, kept short. Capacity sizing is a load question, not a headcount question. What drives it's how many reports render at the same moment and how heavy each one is, so a well-built model on a small A SKU can outserve a bloated model on a big one. That's why the modeling work pays for itself twice: once in trust, once in a smaller capacity bill. You don't need to run this analysis yourself. You need to know it exists and ask whoever quotes you to show the load math at your real usage.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Power BI Embedded cost? The smallest A1 capacity costs about $735 a month when it runs around the clock, and each tier up roughly doubles both the size and the price. Because it bills by the hour, you can shut the capacity down outside business hours and pay much less than the full monthly figure.

Is Power BI Embedded cheaper than buying Power BI Pro seats? It depends on who's looking. For a handful of internal users, the Power BI Pro cost of $14 per seat a month is cheaper and simpler. For hundreds or thousands of outside customers, per-seat licensing doesn't scale and a single Embedded capacity is the cheaper path.

What's the difference between Power BI Embedded and Power BI Premium? Both are capacity-based, but Embedded (A SKUs) is aimed at showing reports to external users inside your own app, while Premium and Fabric capacities are aimed at internal rollouts at scale. Microsoft has merged much of this into Fabric, so ask your vendor which capacity type fits your case.

Can I turn Power BI Embedded off to save money? Yes. A SKUs bill by the hour and can be switched off. If your customers only view reports during the day, shutting it down nights and weekends can cut the bill by more than half. It's the first setting worth checking.

What are the main Power BI license types and cost tiers? Free at $0 for one person, Power BI Pro at $14 per user a month, Power BI Premium Per User at $24 per user a month, Power BI Embedded from about $735 a month as capacity, and Fabric capacities from around $5,000 a month for large rollouts.

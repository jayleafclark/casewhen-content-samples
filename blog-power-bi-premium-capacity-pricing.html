Power BI Premium Capacity Pricing: What You Pay and Where It Goes

Quick answer. Power BI premium capacity pricing means paying for a dedicated pool of compute, a Fabric F SKU, instead of a seat for every person. A P1-equivalent F64 lists at around $5,003 per month on an annual reservation. It starts to pay off once you have roughly 360 or more report viewers. --- You rent a Power BI capacity and the next invoice comes in far higher than you expected, and nobody in the room can say exactly where the money goes. Microsoft now sells the same block of compute under two names at two different prices, and the older figures still sit in older articles, so the number you get quoted depends on which page someone read last. That's how a $5,000 line item lands on an invoice nobody signed off on. This guide lays out the plain Power BI premium capacity pricing math, with the actual dollar numbers, so you can tell in about ten minutes whether capacity saves you money or just costs more.

What's Power BI premium capacity pricing, in plain terms?

You can pay for Power BI in two ways. The first is per person: you buy a seat for each user. The second is capacity: you rent a fixed pool of compute and let as many people read the reports as you want.

Power BI premium capacity pricing is that second route. You commit to a monthly fee for a dedicated block of processing power, whether ten people use it or ten thousand. In 2024 and 2025 Microsoft folded the old Premium "P" SKUs into Microsoft Fabric "F" SKUs, so new buyers now pick an F SKU. The one that matters most is F64. At F64 and above, people who only view reports don't need their own paid seat. Below that line, they still do.

This is the single fact that decides the whole bill. A capacity is a fixed cost, so the value depends on how many read-only people you spread it across. Ten viewers on an F64 is expensive per head. Five thousand viewers on the same F64 is cheap per head. The seats route works the opposite way: it stays flat per person no matter how many you add.

Why does the monthly number feel so hard to pin down?

Because one SKU has two prices. Take F64, the modern stand-in for the old P1:

  • Pay-as-you-go: billed by the hour, about $8,410 per month if you leave it running all month.
  • Reserved: a one-year commitment, about $5,003 per month.

Same compute, very different bill. The reserved price is roughly 40% cheaper, and that gap is where most of the savings come from. Two more things move the number: your region (prices differ across data centers) and whether you turn the capacity off when nobody is using it. A capacity billed by the hour can be switched off overnight to stop the charge, which a reserved plan can't do. The old P1 list price of around $4,995 per month still appears in older articles, which adds to the confusion. When you read a number online, check three things before you trust it: is it the F SKU or the retired P SKU, is it reserved or hourly, and which year is it from.

How do you know when capacity beats per-user seats?

Do the division. F64 reserved is about $5,003 a month. A Power BI Pro seat is about $14 a month. Divide one by the other and you get roughly 357. So the rough crossover is around 360 report viewers.

Under 360 viewers, individual seats are cheaper. Over 360, capacity starts to win, and the more viewers you add the wider the gap gets, because those extra readers cost you nothing on F64.

Here's a worked example. Say you have 500 people who only read reports and 10 people who build them:

  • Seats route: 510 people, and everyone needs a license, so 510 x $14 = $7,140 a month.
  • Capacity route: F64 at $5,003, plus a Pro seat for each of the 10 builders (publishing to a workspace still needs Pro), so $5,003 + $140 = $5,143 a month.

Capacity saves about $2,000 a month here, close to $24,000 a year. It works the other way with a small audience: at 80 viewers instead of 500, the seat route costs 90 x $14 = $1,260, and paying $5,003 for a capacity would be money down the drain. So the count of read-only viewers is the number to check first, before anyone quotes you a SKU.

What do Power BI Pro costs look like next to capacity?

Per-user pricing went up in April 2025. Power BI Pro costs about $14 per user each month now, up from around $10. Premium Per-User, which gives one person the premium features without buying a whole capacity, runs about $24 a month for one person. You'll also see it written as Power BI pro pricing in some places; it's the same $14-a-month plan. The recurring Power BI fees you pay come down to a few options:

OptionRoughly what it costsWho it fits
Power BI Proabout $14 per user / monthsmall teams where most people both build and read
Premium Per-User (PPU)about $24 per user / monthteams that want premium features but stay under ~360 viewers
Fabric F64 (old P1 equivalent)about $5,003 / month reservedlots of viewers who only read reports
Fabric F2 (entry capacity)about $263 / monthdevelopment, testing, and light workloads

The table leaves out one catch: capacity doesn't remove the need for Pro seats for the people who publish. Only the read-only crowd gets in free, and only from F64 up.

How do you avoid paying for capacity you don't use?

A few habits keep the bill predictable.

  1. Start small. An F2 at about $263 a month is plenty for a development or test environment. You don't need F64 to try things out.
  2. Reserve only when the load is steady. Pay-as-you-go is fine while demand is bumpy; switch to the reserved rate once usage settles, and you save that 40%.
  3. Switch off any capacity you rent by the hour when it sits idle. An F SKU you turn off overnight stops charging you until you turn it back on.
  4. Watch the Fabric Capacity Metrics app. It shows when your capacity is throttling, which tells you whether you need a bigger SKU or just a lighter report.
  5. Fix the model before you buy more compute. A slow report is often a bloated data model, not a capacity shortage. In one survey, 67% of organizations said they don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Precisely and Drexel LeBow, 2025), and a bigger SKU won't move that number. A cleaner star schema and incremental refresh usually help more than an extra $3,000 of compute.

Get these five right and the monthly figure stops being hard to predict.

Frequently asked questions

Is Power BI Premium still sold, or is it all Fabric now? New buyers purchase Fabric F SKUs. Microsoft stopped selling the standalone Premium P SKUs to new customers in 2024, though it honored existing agreements through renewal. F64 is the direct replacement for P1.

Do report viewers need a Pro license on a capacity? Only above F64. On an F64 or larger SKU, read-only users can view reports without a paid seat. On anything smaller, every viewer still needs Power BI Pro or PPU.

What's the cheapest way to start with capacity? An F2, at roughly $263 a month pay-as-you-go. It's popular for dev and test work. You can turn it off when you're done for the day to keep the cost down.

How much is Power BI Pro now? About $14 a month per user since the April 2025 increase, which raised it from near $10. Premium Per-User sits at about $24.

Does reserving a capacity really save money? Yes, roughly 40% versus pay-as-you-go for the same SKU. F64 drops from about $8,410 a month to about $5,003 when you commit for a year. Reserve only once your usage is steady.

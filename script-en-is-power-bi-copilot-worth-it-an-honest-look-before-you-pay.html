[0:40-1:05] BEAT 1 — What Copilot actually promises *(ON-SCREEN TEXT card: "Ask in plain English. Get an answer." then SCREEN-RECORD of the demo.)*

CLAIM. Copilot's promise is simple. Anyone can ask a question in plain English and get an answer back, without waiting on the data team.

PROOF. In the demo it looks effortless. Someone types, show me sales by region last quarter, and Copilot writes the query, picks a chart, and drafts a summary line under it. What makes that possible is that Copilot reads your Power BI model, the tables and the measures behind your reports, and turns the plain question into that model's own language.

PAYOFF. So the value is real. When it works, a manager gets an answer in ten seconds that used to take a two-day request to the data team.

RE-HOOK. The catch is in three words I just said. When it works. Here's when it doesn't.

[1:05-2:35] BEAT 2 — Why confident wrong answers *(CUSTOM ANIMATION: two tables, both labelled "revenue", one with tax and one without, Copilot picking one. On-screen card: "Copilot answers. It doesn't check.")*

CLAIM. Copilot is only as good as the model underneath it, and it will give a confident answer even when the model is wrong.

PROOF. Say two tables both have a column called revenue. One includes tax and one doesn't. A person knows which to use. Copilot picks one, gives you a clean number and a tidy chart, and never warns you it chose the wrong one. It doesn't check whether your definition of revenue is agreed across the company. It just answers.

*(DATA-VIZ: a stat card animates in.)* This is why the number matters more than the tool. In one recent survey, close to 40% of CFOs said they don't fully trust their own company's financial data (BlackLine, 2024). Point Copilot at data like that and you get wrong answers faster, with a chart attached.

PAYOFF. So the rule is straightforward. Copilot doesn't clean up a messy model. It speeds it up, in whatever direction it's already pointing.

RE-HOOK. Which raises the question every finance leader should ask before paying. What does this actually cost?

[2:35-4:05] BEAT 3 — What it really costs *(CUSTOM ANIMATION: a capacity meter filling, crossing a line, requests queuing. On-screen card: "Cost moves with usage.")*

CLAIM. Copilot runs on something called Fabric capacity, and the cost moves with how much people use it.

PROOF. Every Copilot question spends compute on that capacity. A few analysts poking at it costs little. Open it to three hundred people asking questions all day and the meter climbs. If the capacity gets busy, requests slow down or queue, and now you're choosing between a laggy experience and buying a bigger, pricier capacity. This is why a Copilot bill can look small in a pilot and then jump once real adoption kicks in.

PAYOFF. So budget Copilot as a usage cost that grows with adoption, and size your Fabric capacity for the busy hour, not the average.

RE-HOOK. That's the bill. The bigger question is whether your data is even ready to earn it back.

[4:05-5:45] BEAT 4 — How to know if your data is ready *(CUSTOM ANIMATION: three data layers cleaning into one agreed number. On-screen card: "Ready = one agreed number.")*

CLAIM. Copilot only pays off on clean, governed data with one agreed number behind it. Most companies aren't there yet.

PROOF. The people who study this are blunt about it. *(DATA-VIZ: stat card.)* In a 2025 study, only 12% of organizations said their data was good enough, in quality and access, to use for AI (Drexel LeBow and Precisely). So if it feels like everyone else already switched Copilot on and won, that's mostly marketing.

*(B-ROLL: a real finance team at work, then back to animation.)* Ready means a few concrete things. One agreed definition for each key measure, revenue, margin, headcount, so there's a single number and not five. Clean tables that relate to each other properly. And access rights set so each person only sees the rows they're allowed to see, which matters the moment Copilot can answer anything for anyone.

PAYOFF. So the readiness check isn't technical trivia. It decides which Copilot you're paying for. The one that gives trusted answers, or the one that gives fast wrong ones.

RE-HOOK. So who's actually getting value from it right now? Some teams are. Let me show you which.

[5:45-7:10] BEAT 5 — Which teams get value today *(TALKING HEAD, then B-ROLL of two contrasting teams. On-screen card: "Ready data earns it. Messy data adds to it.")*

CLAIM. Copilot is worth it today for a narrow group. Teams that already did the boring data work.

PROOF. The finance team that already has one agreed revenue number, a clean model, and access rights set up gets real value. Their people ask questions in plain English and get answers they can take into a meeting. The team that stitched five spreadsheets together last quarter gets confident answers they then have to double-check by hand, which costs more time than it saved.

PAYOFF. So the honest test is your data maturity, not your appetite for AI. Ready data, Copilot earns its cost. Messy data, it adds to your cost.

RE-HOOK. Which means the smart move is getting ready for Copilot before switching it on. Here's what to fix first.

[7:10-8:45] BEAT 6 — What to fix before you pay *(ON-SCREEN TEXT: a numbered list building one item at a time. On-screen card: "Fix these three, in order.")*

CLAIM. Before you pay for Copilot, fix three things, in this order.

PROOF. One, agree the numbers. Pick your key measures and write one definition each, signed off by finance, so revenue means the same thing everywhere. Two, clean the model. Get your tables relating properly and your measures defined once in the model, so Copilot reads a tidy source and not a pile of duplicates. Three, set access rights. Decide who can see which rows before you let anyone ask anything, because Copilot will answer for whoever is typing.

PAYOFF. Do those three and Copilot has something worth reading. Skip them and you're paying a premium to get the same messy answers, only faster.

RE-HOOK. So what does ready actually look like on real data? Here's the picture, and the one question I promised you.

CLAIM. A ready setup has one thing at its center. A single agreed number for every measure that matters.

PROOF. Underneath, clean tables that relate to each other, measures defined once, and access rights that follow each person. On top, Copilot answering plain questions against numbers everyone already trusts. That's when a manager asks, show me margin by product this quarter, and the answer is one you'd put in front of leadership without checking it twice.

If yes, Copilot will likely pay off. If no, fix that first, because Copilot will just give the disagreement a chart. That one question tells you in a minute what a year of invoices would tell you the expensive way.

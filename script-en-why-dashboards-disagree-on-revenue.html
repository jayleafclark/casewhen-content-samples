# Why Do Reports Show Different Numbers? Your Two Dashboards Disagree on Revenue

Script

[0:00-0:12] HOOK (spoken) (MODALITY: talking head, punch-in on the first word) (ON-SCREEN: keyword card, big text, "Why do reports show different numbers") "Your two dashboards disagree on revenue. Same month, same company, two answers. And you found out in front of the board."

[0:12-0:48] PROMISE + STAKES + OPEN LOOP (MODALITY: b-roll, a boardroom, a projected slide, quiet) "If you run finance or data, you've lived this. Slide three says one revenue figure. Slide nine says another. Now the room's arguing about the numbers instead of the decision. By the end of this you'll know the three reasons your reports disagree, and what it takes to get to one number everyone trusts. You don't build the reports yourself, and you don't need to. You need to know what to ask for. And near the end I'll show you the one question that tells you in thirty seconds whether your reporting has an owner or not. Most teams have never asked it." (ON-SCREEN: promise text, "3 reasons + the one question")

[VALUE BEAT 1 - 0:48-2:05] Is one of these numbers just wrong? (ON-SCREEN: chapter card, "Is one of these numbers just wrong?") CLAIM (MODALITY: on-screen text, claim card) "Here's the part that surprises people. Most of the time, both numbers are correct." PROOF (MODALITY: custom animation of the concept) "Watch what happens under the hood. Two reports, both pulling revenue. The first one counts a sale the day the invoice goes out. The second one counts it the day the cash lands. Same sales, same data underneath, two different totals. Now add a second difference. One report includes refunds and credits. The other strips them out. Neither is doing anything wrong. They're answering two slightly different questions, and nobody wrote down which question each report was built to answer." (ON-SCREEN: animation, two pipes from one data source, each ending on a different total, labels "invoice date" vs "cash date") PAYOFF (MODALITY: talking head) "So the first move is not to hunt for the wrong number. The move is to ask what question each report was built to answer. Nine times out of ten, they were answering different ones." RE-HOOK: "Which brings up the bigger problem. Your company doesn't agree on what revenue even means."

[VALUE BEAT 2 - 2:05-3:35] Why does revenue mean three different things here? (ON-SCREEN: chapter card, "Why does revenue mean three things?") CLAIM (MODALITY: on-screen text, claim card) "Ask three departments to define revenue and you'll get three answers." PROOF (MODALITY: data-viz, three definition cards animating in) "Finance defines revenue by accounting rules. Recognized over the contract, net of refunds, tied to the period it belongs in. Sales defines revenue as bookings. The deal closed, the number goes on the board, done. Operations defines it as what shipped or what got delivered. All three are right for what they need. The problem starts when all three build a dashboard, call the metric revenue, and put it in front of you. This is what a data silo looks like from the top. It's not a tools problem. Three teams never agreed on one definition, so the tools faithfully reported three." (ON-SCREEN: three cards, "Finance: recognized", "Sales: bookings", "Ops: delivered", each with a different figure) PAYOFF (MODALITY: talking head) "So the fix is boring and it works. One written definition of revenue, agreed by finance, sales, and ops, before anyone touches a report. Get that on one page and half your disagreements go away." RE-HOOK: "Definitions are one reason. Timing is the second, and it's the one that drags out your month-end without anyone noticing."

[VALUE BEAT 3 - 3:35-5:05] How much is a timing gap slowing your close? (ON-SCREEN: chapter card, "How much is timing costing your close?") CLAIM (MODALITY: on-screen text, claim card) "Two reports can use the same definition and still disagree, because they refreshed at different moments." PROOF (MODALITY: screen record, two report refresh timestamps side by side) "Look at the corner of each report. One refreshed at six this morning. The other refreshed last night at nine. Between those two moments, a big order came in and two returns got processed. So the numbers are hours apart in the real world, and they'll never match while they refresh on different clocks. Now scale that up. Your finance team is closing the month, and half the source data is still updating while they pull reports. That's a chunk of why closes run long. Solvexia puts a solid close at around eight days, and the fastest finance teams get under three. A lot of the gap between those two is people reconciling numbers that were only ever captured at different times." (ON-SCREEN: two timestamps, "refreshed 06:00" and "refreshed 21:00 yesterday", a callout on the order that landed between them) PAYOFF (MODALITY: b-roll, a clock, a calendar page turning) "So when two numbers disagree, check the refresh time before you check anything else. And for the close, agree one cut-off. Everyone reports as of the same moment, or the reconciling never ends." RE-HOOK: "Definitions and timing you can fix in a week. This next one is the reason they come back."

[VALUE BEAT 4 - 5:05-6:35] Who really owns the number? (ON-SCREEN: chapter card, "Who really owns the number?") CLAIM (MODALITY: on-screen text, claim card) "Most reporting messes trace back to one thing. No single person owns the number." PROOF (MODALITY: custom animation of the concept) "Watch how a metric drifts with no owner. Someone in sales builds a revenue view for a QBR. Someone in finance builds their own for the board. A regional lead copies one, tweaks a filter, and now there's a fourth. Every version is used by real people making real decisions, and no one is responsible for keeping them in agreement. So they drift apart, month after month. Gartner found 47 percent of digital workers can't easily find the data they need to do their job. When you sit where you sit, that makes sense, because when no one owns a number, no one can point people to the version they should be using." (ON-SCREEN: animation, one metric copied into four diverging tiles, each with a slightly different value, no name attached to any) PAYOFF (MODALITY: talking head) "So here's that thirty-second question I promised. Point at your revenue number and ask, who owns this definition, and who signs off when it changes. If the room goes quiet, that's your answer. An unowned number will disagree with itself forever." RE-HOOK: "Now, the part where I get a little technical, because you should know what good looks like under the reports."

[VALUE BEAT 5 - 6:35-8:15] What does your data team do to fix this? (ON-SCREEN: chapter card, "What does your team do about it?") CLAIM (MODALITY: on-screen text, claim card, "The one technical part") "This is the one spot I'll go under the hood, then I'll pull straight back out." PROOF (MODALITY: custom animation of a semantic model) "Good teams stop defining revenue inside each report. Instead they define it once, in a shared layer that sits between your raw data and every dashboard. In Power BI that shared layer is called the semantic model. Revenue gets written down there one time, with the refund rule, the timing rule, all of it. Then every report, every board slide, and even a Copilot question, they all pull from that one definition. Change it once and it changes everywhere. That's the whole game. One definition, one refresh schedule, one owner, feeding everything above it. When people ask what a single source of truth means in plain terms, that's it. Not a new tool. One agreed definition that every report is forced to use." (ON-SCREEN: animation, one central "revenue defined here" block feeding many dashboards and a Copilot chat, all showing the same figure) PAYOFF (MODALITY: talking head) "You don't need to build that. You need to ask your team one thing. Is revenue defined in one shared place that every report pulls from, or is each report defining it on its own. That answer tells you how much work you're really looking at." RE-HOOK: "So what does it look like when this is done right and you walk into the board?"

[VALUE BEAT 6 / PAYOFF SETUP - 8:15-9:45] What does one trusted number look like in a board meeting? (ON-SCREEN: chapter card, "What does one trusted number look like?") CLAIM (MODALITY: on-screen text, claim card) "When it's done right, the number stops being the topic. The decision becomes the topic." PROOF (MODALITY: b-roll, a settled board meeting, then data-viz of a single clean figure) "Slide three and slide nine show the same revenue, because they pull from the same definition. Nobody opens a laptop to check. When someone asks how it's calculated, there's one written answer and one name attached to it. Your close comes in days shorter, because finance isn't reconciling three versions anymore, they're reporting one. And when the board asks a follow-up, you answer the question instead of defending the figure. That's the difference this makes. Reporting the room believes on sight. Around 70 percent of dashboard work goes unused because people don't trust it, according to RevealBI. Fix trust and that wasted work turns into decisions." (ON-SCREEN: data-viz, one revenue figure, a small "defined once, owned by finance" tag under it) PAYOFF (MODALITY: kinetic type, recap list building) "So here's the whole thing in four lines. Recap When two revenue numbers disagree, both are usually correct. They're answering different questions. One report counts a sale at invoice, the other at cash. One includes refunds, the other strips them out. On top of that, three departments define revenue three ways, and reports refresh at different times, so the totals drift. The deeper reason it keeps happening is that no single person owns the definition. Good teams define revenue once, in a shared semantic model that every report and every Copilot answer pulls from, with one refresh cut-off and one owner. That's what a single source of truth means in practice. Agree the definition, set the cut-off, name the owner, and your board reporting starts to reconcile on its own.

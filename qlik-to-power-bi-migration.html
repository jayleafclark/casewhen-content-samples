Qlik to Power BI Migration: How to Move Without Breaking Your Numbers

Quick Answer: A qlik to Power BI migration is the project of rebuilding your QlikView or Qlik Sense reports, data models, and security inside Power BI, then retiring Qlik. No tool converts one into the other, because Power BI and Qlik model data differently, so the work is to redefine each number, rebuild the model as a star schema, and reset permissions so the reports match what people trusted before. Done in the right order, users keep their numbers and you drop the Qlik licence.

You have decided to move off Qlik, and now the real worry starts: the reports people rely on every day were built over years, and if the new Power BI versions show even slightly different numbers, nobody will trust them. A qlik to Power BI migration is where most of that risk lives, because Qlik and Power BI do not store or calculate data the same way. Copy the dashboards across without rethinking the model underneath and you will spend the next three months explaining why last quarter's revenue changed.

The reason this happens is simple. Qlik uses an associative engine and its own load script to pull and shape data. Power BI uses a semantic model, relationships, and a language called DAX. There is no button that turns one into the other. Every measure, every filter, and every security rule has to be redefined by hand. That rebuild work is the whole project, and treating it as a straight copy is the single most common reason these projects stall with both tools running at once.

Why do teams move from Qlik to Power BI?

The trigger is usually cost and consolidation. Qlik licences and the servers behind QlikView or Qlik Sense add up, and most companies are already paying for Microsoft 365, which makes Power BI far cheaper per user. When finance looks at two BI bills, one of them tends to lose.

The second reason is the stack. If your data is heading into Azure or Microsoft Fabric, Power BI sits inside that world natively. Refreshes, security, and Copilot all connect without extra plumbing. Keeping Qlik on the side means maintaining a separate tool, separate skills, and a separate support contract for the same job.

The third reason is people. Power BI skills are easier to hire for than Qlik skills, and the analysts you already have are more likely to know it. When the one person who understands your Qlik load scripts leaves, the risk of staying on Qlik becomes very real.

What is actually different between Qlik and Power BI?

The gap that breaks migrations is under the hood, not on the screen. Qlik's associative model lets any field relate to any other field, and its load script does the data shaping up front. Power BI wants a defined model: fact tables, dimension tables, and clear relationships, usually arranged as a star schema. A Qlik app that leaned on the associative engine to avoid building a clean model will not translate cleanly, because Power BI needs that structure to give correct answers.

Calculations move too. Qlik expressions and set analysis do not exist in Power BI. Every one becomes a DAX measure, and DAX handles filter context in its own way. A number that was correct in Qlik can come back wrong in Power BI simply because the filter behaves differently, even when the formula looks the same. This is why testing against known figures matters more than rebuilding quickly.

Security is the third difference. Qlik section access controls who sees what. Power BI uses row-level security, defined in the model and tied to the user. It does the same job, but the rules have to be rewritten, not imported, and a missed rule means a regional manager sees the whole company or sees nothing.

Why do numbers change during a qlik to Power BI migration?

This is the fear that keeps Heads of Data up at night, and it is worth being precise about the causes.

The same number was defined more than once in Qlik. Many Qlik environments grew report by report, so revenue exists as several slightly different expressions across apps. When you rebuild in Power BI you have to pick one definition, and whichever you pick, some old report will now disagree with the new one. The migration surfaces a disagreement that was already sitting in the data, and fixing it once during the move is far cheaper than living with it forever.

Filter context works differently. A Qlik set analysis expression and a DAX measure can produce different results for month-to-date or year-over-year figures because each tool decides what to include in its own way. If you port the formula without testing the result, the difference shows up in front of the business.

Data types and joins shift. Dates stored as text, keys that were loosely matched in the load script, and duplicate rows that the associative engine absorbed on its own all behave differently once they sit in a Power BI star schema. The fix is to clean and define them during the move, not after someone spots a wrong total.

The lesson across all three: rebuild the definitions deliberately, then check every migrated report against a Qlik number you already trust before anyone else sees it.

Is the migration worth the disruption?

It is, but only if enough people actually use the reports on the other side. That is the part teams forget. On average only 25% of employees use the BI tools their company pays for, and that figure has stayed flat for seven years, according to BARC and Eckerson Group in their 2022 research. If your Qlik reports were part of that unused majority, lifting them into Power BI unchanged just moves the problem. The migration is the moment to cut the reports nobody opens, fix the ones people fought to trust, and give the business fewer, cleaner numbers. Done that way, adoption goes up and the licence saving is only half the return.

What is the right order for a qlik to Power BI migration?

You do not rebuild everything at once. You move in a sequence that protects trust at each step.

Start by taking inventory. List every Qlik app, who uses it, how often, and which numbers it drives. Kill the reports with no real users now, before you spend a day rebuilding them. This step alone usually shrinks the work by a third.

Agree the core numbers next. Pick the ten or so measures leadership actually asks about, revenue, margin, active customers, pipeline, and write one agreed definition for each. This is the work that stops numbers from changing later, and it is the foundation the whole migration stands on.

Rebuild the data model as a star schema. Set up the fact and dimension tables and the relationships so Power BI has a clean structure to calculate against. Recreate the Qlik expressions as DAX measures against that model, one at a time.

Reset security with row-level security. Translate every Qlik section access rule into a Power BI rule and test each role against a known user before go-live.

Validate against Qlik, then switch. Run the new Power BI reports side by side with the live Qlik ones and reconcile every key number until they tie out. Only then move users over, app by app, and retire the Qlik licence once the last report is confirmed.

FAQ

Can I convert QlikView or Qlik Sense files directly into Power BI? No. There is no reliable file converter, and anything that claims to be one only copies the visuals, not the model or the calculations. A qlik to Power BI migration means rebuilding the data model, the measures, and the security in Power BI by hand. That sounds like more work, and it is, but it is also the only way the numbers come out matching what people trusted in Qlik.

How long does a qlik to Power BI migration take? It depends on how many apps you actually keep, not how many you have. Once you cut the unused reports and agree your core numbers, a focused migration of the reports that matter usually runs in weeks per group of apps, not months for everything at once. Moving in waves, validating each one against Qlik before switching users over, is faster and far safer than a single big cutover.

Will my numbers be the same in Power BI as they were in Qlik? They will if you rebuild the definitions deliberately and test them. Numbers change when a measure was defined several ways in Qlik, or when filter context works differently in DAX. The fix is to agree one definition for each key number, then reconcile every migrated report against a Qlik figure you already trust before anyone else sees it.

Governance · Austin Levine

Data governance framework: why two people pull the same number and get two different answers

Quick answer. A data governance framework is a short set of written rules for your company's numbers. For each important number, the rules say what it means, who is in charge of it, where the meaning is written down, and how someone is allowed to change it. When these rules exist, everyone who looks up that number gets the same answer. When they do not, two people can look up the same number and get two different answers, and both of them are technically correct.

Most people think a wrong number in a board report is a formula error. In almost every review we run, the formula is correct. The problem is that nobody agreed on what the number is supposed to mean, and nobody was put in charge of deciding.

Here is what that looks like in practice. Sales reports last month's revenue as one figure. Finance reports a smaller figure because they took out returns and credit notes. Operations reports a third figure because they counted orders by the date they shipped instead of the date they were invoiced. All three teams did the math correctly, and the three numbers still do not match, because nobody told them which definition of revenue is the official one.

Why does the same number come out two different ways?

Because two people defined it differently and nobody wrote down which definition is correct. Once two versions are live in Power BI, the reports will disagree until someone decides which version is the correct one and writes it down. When two of those numbers reach the same meeting, the more senior person's version usually wins.

Do you need Microsoft Fabric to get governance right?

No. Fabric changes what you pay Microsoft for computing power. It has nothing to do with whether your numbers have owners and definitions. If a consultant tells you the answer is a platform upgrade, they are selling you the upgrade.

How do you know how good your governance is?
StageWhat it looks likeThe test
FragileNo named owners, changes go straight to the live reportNobody can name the owner of the board dataset
FunctionalOwners assigned, definitions written downA change still breaks a report nobody expected
Board-readyDev, test and live reports, versioned changes, RLS re-testedA wrong number is caught before the board sees it

A green status light on a report means the data loaded on schedule. It says nothing about whether the number itself is correct. We watched a board approve a cash balance three times too high under a green checkmark.

KPI & Datenmodellierung · Sajagan Thirugnanam

Bedeutung KPI: eine Definition, die im Meeting standhält

Quick answer. Die Bedeutung von KPI ist schnell erklärt: eine KPI ist eine Kennzahl mit einem klaren Ziel und einem Besitzer. Was sie wirklich bedeutet, hängt an der Definition und am Besitzer. Fehlt eines davon, berichtet jede Abteilung dieselbe KPI anders.

Der häufigste Irrtum: eine KPI ist keine Metrik ohne Ziel. "Umsatz" ist eine Metrik. "Umsatz gegen Plan pro Monat" ist eine KPI, weil ein Ziel und ein Zeitbezug dranhängen.

Warum hat dieselbe KPI in drei Systemen drei Werte?

Weil drei Personen sie unterschiedlich definiert haben, und keine Definition ist aufgeschrieben. Der "Umsatz letzter Monat" kann brutto oder netto sein, nach Rechnungs- oder Lieferdatum. Alle drei Werte sind richtig, keiner ist abgestimmt. Genau hier entsteht die falsche Zahl im Aufsichtsrat, und in Deutschland ist das eine Haftungsfrage.

Wie definiert man eine KPI, die standhält?

Auf einer Seite, in fünf Feldern: Name, Formel, Quelle, Besitzer, Stand. Diese Seite ist das eigentliche Produkt. In Power BI gehört die Definition ins zentrale Datenmodell als Measure, damit jeder Bericht denselben Wert zeigt.

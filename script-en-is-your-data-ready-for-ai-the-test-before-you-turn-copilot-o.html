[0:52-2:08] BEAT 1 — What does "AI-ready data" actually mean?

[Custom animation: a chat question flowing into a semantic model layer, then out to tables] CLAIM: Copilot is only as good as the model sitting underneath it.

[Screen-record: Power BI, showing a semantic model with measures and relationships] PROOF: Here's the part most people miss. When someone asks Copilot a question, it doesn't read your raw tables and work it out. It reads your semantic model, which is the layer where your measures, your table names, and your relationships live. If "Net Revenue" is defined there as a proper measure, Copilot uses it. If it isn't defined, Copilot guesses from whatever columns it can find, and it will still answer with a straight face.

[On-screen text, numbered build] PAYOFF: So "AI-ready data" comes down to four plain checks. One, your key number comes from one place. Two, Copilot can reach the data and only the rows each person is allowed to see. Three, your model is described in business words, not database codes. Four, it's refreshed and someone owns it. Get those four right and Copilot has something solid to stand on.

[Talking head] RE-HOOK: Let's take them one at a time, starting with the one that sinks most rollouts.

ON-SCREEN CARD: "Check 1 — one number, one source"

[2:08-3:34] BEAT 2 — Does your top number come from one place?

[Talking head] CLAIM: If two of your reports disagree about revenue today, Copilot will pick one of them and sound certain about it.

[Custom animation: two report tiles showing €4.2M and €4.6M, an arrow, Copilot returning one of them] PROOF: This is the everyday version of the problem. Your sales report counts revenue on the booking date. Your finance report counts it on the invoice date. Same company, same quarter, two different numbers, and both are defended by the people who built them. A human in a meeting can spot which one the question needs. Copilot can't. It grabs the first matching measure it finds and reports it as the answer. So the disagreement you already had between two teams now gets broadcast to everyone in the business, in plain English, at machine speed.

[On-screen text] PAYOFF: The fix comes before any AI. Take your most important metric and give it one agreed definition, in one model, that both teams sign off on. This is what we call the one agreed number. When people already get the same figure from the same place, Copilot inherits that, and it stops guessing.

[Talking head] RE-HOOK: Good. Now, even a clean number is useless if the tool can't reach it, or reaches too much of it.

ON-SCREEN CARD: "Check 2 — access and row-level security"

[3:34-5:00] BEAT 3 — Can Copilot see your data, and only the right rows?

[Screen-record: Fabric / OneLake showing data in a governed workspace vs a lone spreadsheet on a desktop] CLAIM: Copilot can only answer from data it can actually reach, and it has to respect who's allowed to see what.

[Custom animation: row-level security — one report, two users, rows filtering per identity] PROOF: Two things go wrong here. First, reach. If half your real numbers live in a spreadsheet on someone's laptop, Copilot never sees them, so it answers from the stale copy in the warehouse and nobody realises the gap. Second, permissions. Say a regional manager asks Copilot for the top earners on the team. Without row-level security in place, the tool happily returns everyone's salary across the whole company, because the data was there and nothing told it to filter. Row-level security means each person only ever gets the rows tied to their identity, whether they ask in a dashboard or in a chat box.

[On-screen text] PAYOFF: So check two is simple to state. Your data lives in one governed place, and every person's permissions are set at the data layer, not hidden inside individual reports. If access is right for people, it's right for Copilot, because Copilot answers as that person.

[Talking head] RE-HOOK: Reach and permissions sorted. Now the check that decides whether Copilot even understands the question.

ON-SCREEN CARD: "Check 3 — business words, not codes"

[5:00-6:24] BEAT 4 — Does your model use business words or database codes?

[Screen-record: a table with columns named REV_NET_EUR_FIN_V3, then renamed to "Net Revenue" with a description] CLAIM: Copilot maps a plain-English question to your data using the names and descriptions you gave your fields.

[Screen-record continues: adding a description and synonyms to a measure] PROOF: Look at a real column name. REV_NET_EUR_FIN_V3. You know that means net revenue. Copilot has no idea. So when someone asks "what was our revenue", it may skip that column entirely and add up something that looks closer to the word, and now the answer is wrong for a reason nobody can see. The repair is unglamorous and it works: give your measures friendly names, write a one-line description of what each one means, and add the words your business actually says, so "revenue", "sales", and "turnover" all point at the same measure.

[On-screen text] PAYOFF: Check three: name and describe your top twenty measures in business language. This is a week of unglamorous work, and it does more for Copilot accuracy than any prompt trick you'll read about online.

[Talking head] RE-HOOK: Those are the four checks. Before I give you the fastest way to test all of this at once, let me be straight about what skipping it costs.

ON-SCREEN CARD: "The one-question readiness test"

[7:52-9:14] BEAT 6 — The one question that reveals your AI readiness

[Talking head, single locked shot, quiet] CLAIM: You don't need a big assessment to know if you're ready. You need one question, asked of two people.

[Custom animation: same question routed to two colleagues, two different sources light up] PROOF: Here it is. Pick your single most important metric. Revenue, active customers, margin, whatever your business runs on. Now go to two different people who report on it and ask them where the real number comes from. Which report, which measure, which definition. If you get one answer, your foundation is solid and Copilot has something true to repeat. If you get two answers, you aren't ready, and no amount of AI will settle the argument, it will only spread it. This one question beats every vendor demo, because demos run on clean sample data that was built to look perfect. Your data has history.

[On-screen text] PAYOFF: That's the test. One metric, two people, one question: where does the real number come from? The answer tells you in five minutes what a long audit would tell you in five weeks.

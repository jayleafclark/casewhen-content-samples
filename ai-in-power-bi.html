AI in Power BI only works on data that's ready for it

Quick Answer: AI in Power BI means Copilot and the Fabric AI features that let people ask questions in plain English and get answers, summaries, and draft reports. It only gives right answers when the data underneath is clean, governed, and comes from one agreed source. So the first thing to sort out is whether your data can support it. ---

You keep hearing that AI in Power BI will answer questions in plain English, so you turn on Copilot, ask it for last month's revenue, and get a number nobody in the room can check. It sounds confident. It looks finished. And you have no fast way to tell if it's right. That's the gap this piece is about, and most teams hit it in the first week.

The reason is simple. Copilot doesn't invent a correct number. It reads whatever model and tables you point it at. If two of your reports already disagree on revenue, the AI will happily pick one, phrase it well, and hand it to you as fact. So the tool inherits every problem your data already has, and then says it with more confidence than a human would.

What does AI in Power BI actually do today?

There are two pieces people mean when they say AI in Power BI, and it helps to separate them.

The first is Copilot inside Power BI. You type a question like "show me margin by product for last quarter" and it builds the visual, or it writes a first draft of a report page, or it summarizes what a dashboard is showing in a sentence. It also drafts the underlying calculations for you.

The second is the Microsoft Fabric AI layer underneath. Fabric is where your data is stored, cleaned, and joined together before a report ever sees it. The AI features there help prepare and connect data, and they're what let Copilot answer across your whole business rather than one small dataset.

Both are useful. Neither can fix a number that was wrong before it arrived. That's the part the demos skip.

Why can't you just switch Copilot on?

Because Copilot answers from your model, and most models were never built to be asked open questions.

Think about how a normal report gets made. Someone loads the tables that were easy to get, wires up a few visuals for a specific meeting, and ships it. It answers the three questions it was built for. Copilot, though, gets asked anything: margin by customer, revenue by region, headcount by month. When the model wasn't built to answer that, the AI still returns something, and now you have a confident answer with no owner and no way to trace it.

This is the honest message, and it's worth saying plainly. You can't just switch AI in Power BI on and trust it. Your data has to be ready first. Ready means the numbers are defined once, the tables are joined correctly, permissions are set so people only see what they should, and every report reads from the same source.

Only 12% of organizations say their data is good enough, in both quality and access, to actually use for AI (Drexel LeBow and Precisely, 2025). So if your setup isn't ready yet, you're in the large majority, not behind.

What does "AI ready" data actually mean?

It's less exotic than it sounds. AI ready data is the same clean foundation that makes any report trustworthy, and Copilot just raises the stakes because it will answer far more questions than a human ever coded by hand.

Here's what changes when you point AI at a messy setup versus a governed one.

The left column is why a Copilot pilot gets abandoned. People try it, get an answer they can't verify, and go back to the spreadsheet they believe. The right column is what makes it stick, and it's the same work that fixes reports for humans too.

What does it cost when the data isn't ready?

The visible cost is a wrong decision made quickly. Say your head of sales asks Copilot for pipeline by region before a forecast call, gets a clean-looking answer built on a bad join, and the quarterly forecast goes out too high. It gets walked back a month later, and the tool that was meant to save time just made a confident mistake faster.

The quieter cost is the pilot that dies. You paid for Copilot licenses, a few people tried it, nobody could trust the answers, and now the license sits unused while everyone keeps working the old way. You've bought AI and kept the manual work.

There's also a permissions cost people forget. Copilot answers from what the model can see. If your model has no row-level security, a plain-English question can surface numbers a person was never meant to reach, like another team's margins or a salary line. On messy data, the AI is a fast way to leak the wrong figure to the wrong person.

What should you fix before turning on AI in Power BI?

You don't need a huge program. You need the data foundation in order first, then the AI on top.

  1. Agree your core numbers once. Revenue, margin, and a couple of others, each defined in writing and built into one shared model, so there's a single answer for Copilot to find.
  2. Point every report at that one model instead of separate extracts. This is what stops Copilot picking between five versions of the same figure.
  3. Turn on row-level security so each person, and the AI answering for them, only sees the data they're allowed to.
  4. Name an owner for each model and report. An AI answer with no human owner is where trust breaks.
  5. Then, and only then, switch on Copilot for a small group and check its answers against the trusted reports before you widen it.

Do those four foundation steps and the AI stops guessing, because there's one clean, governed, single source for it to read. This is exactly the Microsoft Fabric AI and Power BI Copilot readiness work we do, and it's mostly modeling and governance work.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need Microsoft Fabric to use AI in Power BI? Basic Copilot inside a single report doesn't need it. The bigger AI features, and the ability to answer questions spanning your whole business, run on the Fabric AI layer. It's where data gets prepared, so it matters more the more you want the AI to do.

Will Copilot fix our conflicting numbers on its own? No. Copilot reads your model and answers from it. If two reports disagree today, it will pick one and state it confidently. The fix is one agreed number in a shared model first, then the AI reads that.

Is our data ready for AI right now? Most teams' data isn't, and that's normal. Ready means numbers defined once, tables joined correctly, permissions set with row-level security, and every report reading from the same source. A short review tells you where the gaps are.

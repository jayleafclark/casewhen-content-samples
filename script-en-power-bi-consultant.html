(CLAIM CARD on-screen: "You pay for the model, not the pixels.")

CUSTOM ANIMATION (data-viz, the day split into hours): a single horizontal bar labelled "1 day = 463 pounds" fills left to right, then splits into segments that animate in one at a time with their share of the day: requirements and discovery (about 15 percent), data model and relationships (about 30 percent), DAX measures (about 20 percent), row-level security and access (about 15 percent), refresh setup (about 10 percent), and last a small segment for visuals (about 10 percent). Keep it in CaseWhen green with one amber marker on the visuals slice.

> Add it up and about three quarters of the day is model work, measures, security and refresh. The dashboard is the part you see, and it's the cheapest part to make.

(PAYOFF, numbered takeaway on-screen: "1. Judge a consultant on the model and the measures, not the dashboard.") > Takeaway one. When you price a consultant, you're mostly paying for the model and the measures, so judge them on those, not on how tidy the dashboard looks.

[2:05-3:25] VALUE BEAT 2: "Is it the reports or the model underneath?" · MODALITY: screen-record > Slow reports and totals that don't reconcile almost always trace back to one thing, a single wide flat table.

(CLAIM CARD: "Slow and wrong usually means one flat table.")

SCREEN-RECORD (real Power BI Desktop): open a report built on one flat table of 12 million rows. Hit refresh. An on-screen timer climbs, memory usage spikes near the top of the meter, the refresh lands at about 40 minutes. Then switch to a second file where the same data is rebuilt as a star schema. Drop into Model view.

CUSTOM ANIMATION (star schema, over the model view): a central fact table sits in the middle, then date, product, customer and region dimension tables slide in around it and snap into place, with one-to-many arrows lighting up green as each relationship connects. Then cut back to the screen-record and hit refresh on the star-schema version: the timer lands at about 4 minutes. Same data, same rows.

> Same 12 million rows. One flat table refreshes in 40 minutes and the numbers drift between reports. The star schema refreshes in about 4 and every measure reads off one agreed structure. This is also why manual reports go wrong so often. A peer-reviewed 2024 study by Poon and colleagues found 94 percent of business spreadsheets used in decisions contain errors.

(PAYOFF card: "2. A 40-minute refresh or totals that disagree is a modeling job.") > Takeaway two. If your refresh takes 40 minutes, or your totals disagree across reports, that's a modeling job, and it's the highest-value thing a consultant does for you.

RE-HOOK: > Which brings up the question you came here for. When is paying someone worth it?

[3:25-4:50] VALUE BEAT 3: "When does hiring one pay for itself?" · MODALITY: custom animation > Three situations make a consultant pay for itself quickly. Watch for these.

(CLAIM CARD: "Three triggers where a consultant pays back.")

CUSTOM ANIMATION (three panels build in sequence, each with its own label): - Panel 1, "A hard deadline". A calendar with a fixed date circled, say a Tableau licence ending or a Power BI Premium P-SKU migration deadline in 2026 or 2028. A work bar tries to fit before the date and overflows past it, then a second bar with a consultant fits inside. Label: "fixed date, work won't fit". - Panel 2, "One number that has to be right". Two dashboards side by side show 4.2 million and 4.6 million for the same month. A red mismatch marker blinks. Then they converge to a single reconciled figure. Label: "finance and ops read different numbers". - Panel 3, "A model past your depth". Show incremental refresh partitions as a row of month blocks, with only the newest block reprocessing and glowing while the older archived blocks stay cold and still. Alongside it, small icons for DAX time intelligence and row-level security. Label: "the parts most teams haven't built before".

> A hard date you can't move. A number that has to reconcile and doesn't. And a modeling job past your team's depth, things like incremental refresh, row-level security, or time-intelligence DAX. Any one of those, and a few days of the right person saves you weeks.

(PAYOFF card: "3. Hire on a hard deadline, a number that must reconcile, or a model past your depth.") > Takeaway three. Bring in a consultant when there's a hard deadline, a number that has to reconcile, or a modeling job past your team's depth. Those three pay back fast.

RE-HOOK: > The flip side matters just as much. Here's when that same spend gives you nothing.

[4:50-6:15] VALUE BEAT 4: "When are you just spending for nothing?" · MODALITY: b-roll + on-screen checklist > Two situations turn a consultant into pure cost.

(CLAIM CARD: "Two ways to burn a day rate for nothing.")

B-ROLL (real office footage: a full meeting room, sticky notes, a wall of near-identical report tabs) with an ON-SCREEN CHECKLIST animating item by item: - Item 1 appears: "Building 30 dashboards nobody agreed on." Show duplicate reports stacking up and workspace names sprawling down the screen. Voiceover explains that without agreed definitions and clear owners, more reports just means more disagreement, now at 463 pounds a day. - Item 2 appears: "Outsourcing a one-off you could learn." Show a single chart type and one basic measure. Voiceover: that's a training gap, cheaper to take a course or read the docs than to rent a specialist for an afternoon.

> Paying a day rate to mass-produce reports without agreed definitions gives you sprawl at a premium. And renting a specialist to do a one-off you could learn yourself is money you didn't need to spend.

(PAYOFF card: "4. Don't pay a day rate for undefined report sprawl or a one-off you could learn.") > Takeaway four. Don't pay a day rate to churn out reports with no agreed definitions, and don't outsource a one-off you could learn in an afternoon.

RE-HOOK: > Say it is worth it. Then you hit the next question, how you pay, because that decides your final bill more than the rate does.

[6:15-7:40] VALUE BEAT 5: "Day rate or fixed scope, which do you ask for?" · MODALITY: data-viz > How you buy the work changes your total more than the day rate does.

(CLAIM CARD: "How you buy it beats what it costs per day.")

CUSTOM ANIMATION (data-viz, two paths race down the screen): - Path A, "Open-ended day rate": 463 pounds per day times an unknown number of days. A bar grows downward with a blurred, undefined end and a question mark, and a running total ticks up past the estimate. - Path B, "Fixed scope, fixed price": a bounded box with an agreed deliverable listed inside, a working star schema, five certified measures, row-level security by region, and a refresh under 5 minutes. One price, one date, the box stays the same size no matter how long it takes. Animate the day-rate total overshooting while the fixed-scope box stays flat.

> On an open-ended day rate, every overrun is on your bill. On a fixed scope tied to a deliverable, the risk of it taking longer sits with the people doing the work. For a defined job, that's the cheaper deal almost every time.

(PAYOFF card: "5. For a defined job, buy a fixed scope tied to a deliverable, not open-ended days.") > Takeaway five. For a defined job, ask for a fixed scope and a fixed price tied to a deliverable, not an open-ended day rate. It moves the risk of overrun onto the people doing the build.

RE-HOOK: > So what does a sane engagement look like in real numbers? Here's one.

[7:40-9:00] WORKED EXAMPLE / REAL NUMBER: "What does a sane engagement look like?" · MODALITY: screen-record + b-roll > Take a finance team whose month-end runs on manual exports, where revenue won't reconcile between two reports.

SCREEN-RECORD + B-ROLL: show the before state, a manual export being copied between files, and a refresh timer sitting at 40 minutes, and two dashboards showing different revenue for the same month. Then show the after state: Model view of a clean star schema, five certified measures, row-level security by region, and incremental refresh switched on. Refresh timer now lands near 4 minutes, and both reports show one agreed figure.

(ON-SCREEN before/after figure: "Refresh 40 min → ~4 min · two numbers → one · scope: 6 to 8 days")

> Scope that properly and it's roughly 6 to 8 days of work. At the median UK rate that's about 3,000 to 3,700 pounds. Set that against the fact that only 23.9 percent of large firms call themselves data-driven, according to NewVantage in 2023, and against what Thomas Redman found, that defective input data costs about ten times as much per unit of work as clean data does. Companies that run on trusted numbers are 8.5 times more likely to report 20 percent or better revenue growth year on year, according to Forrester. A few thousand pounds to make month-end reconcile is not the expensive line here.

(B-roll: the finance lead nodding at a single clean dashboard on a wall screen.)

Business Intelligence And Consulting vs. Big Consultancies: What's Actually Different

Quick answer. Business intelligence and consulting means paying an outside firm to turn your messy data into reports your team trusts and acts on. A specialist firm and a big consultancy both sell it, but they price it, staff it, and finish it very differently. For most mid-sized companies, a specialist gets you a trusted setup faster and for less. ---

Business intelligence and consulting is where you hire an outside team to get your numbers clean, defined once, and trusted enough that people actually make decisions on them. The reason this keeps coming up is money. Poor data quality costs organizations $12.9M a year on average (Gartner), and 67% of companies say they don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Precisely). So the market for help is huge, and two very different kinds of firm are selling into it: big brand-name consultancies, and smaller specialists who do one thing.

This piece is about what's actually different between them, in the terms you're judged on: cost, speed, who does the work, and whether the thing gets finished. No pitch. Just where each one wins and where each one hurts.

What is business intelligence and consulting, really?

Strip away the brochure language and it's simple. You have data sitting in systems, spreadsheets, and someone's inbox, and you can't get a straight answer out of it. A firm comes in, builds a proper foundation under your reports, and leaves you with numbers that come out the same way every time.

The reason it's worth paying for is that this is harder than it looks. Around 70% of dashboard work is wasted because people don't trust or use what gets built (RevealBI). You can spend real money and still end up with reports the team ignores and rebuilds in Excel. So what you're really buying isn't a dashboard. It's the trust. A number the board believes, a close you can finish on time, a meeting that doesn't turn into an argument about whose figure is right.

Both a big consultancy and a specialist firm will tell you they deliver that. The difference is in how they're built.

How is a big consultancy different from a specialist?

The short version: a big consultancy sells you a brand and a process, and a specialist sells you the people who do the work.

At a large firm, the partner who wins the deal is rarely the person building your reports. That work usually rolls down to junior staff who rotate on and off projects. You pay senior rates, and a lot of the day-to-day gets done by people early in their careers, learning on your engagement. The methodology is real, but it's built for a company ten times your size, so you get process weight you don't need.

A specialist power bi consulting firm is the opposite shape. The person who scopes the job is often the person who builds it, or sits right next to them. There are fewer layers, so less of your money pays for management overhead and slide decks. You lose the big brand name on the invoice. You gain someone who has built the same kind of trusted setup many times and can get you there in weeks.

Neither is automatically right. It depends on what you're buying and how fast you need it.

What does a big consultancy actually cost you?

More than the rate card, and in ways that show up later.

The obvious cost is the day rate, which is high because you're paying for the brand and the overhead behind it. The less obvious cost is the ramp. A rotating team spends billed time learning your business, your definitions, and your data before they build anything useful. You pay full rates during that stretch, and if the team rotates again mid-project, you pay for parts of it twice.

Then there's the finish problem. Big engagements are good at starting and documenting. They're less good at the last mile, where a report has to actually get used every day by a controller who doesn't care about the methodology. When the team rolls off, the knowledge can roll off with them, and you're left with a handsome deliverable nobody on your side can maintain. That's how you end up back where you started: 82% of companies say data silos disrupt their workflows (ZoomInfo), and a half-finished project becomes another silo.

None of this means a big firm does bad work. It means your total cost is the rate plus the ramp plus the risk that it doesn't land, and only the first part shows up in the quote.

When does a big firm still make sense?

When the job really is enterprise-sized and the brand matters.

If you're a very large organization running a multi-year transformation across dozens of countries and systems, you need the scale, the legal cover, and the sheer headcount a big consultancy brings. A specialist team of a few people can't staff that, and shouldn't try.

There's also the credibility case. Sometimes a board or a regulator wants a recognized name on the work, and that name is part of what you're buying. That's a real reason, and it's worth being honest about when it applies to you and when it's just habit.

For most mid-sized companies, though, the reporting problem isn't enterprise-sized. It's a semantic model that was never built right, a close that takes too long, and a board pack that doesn't reconcile. That's a specialist job, and paying enterprise rates for it is how budgets get burned.

The pattern that works for most companies your size is to skip the big-firm overhead and hire a specialist to build the trusted foundation fast. You get a working result in weeks instead of a project that ramps for a quarter. If you really do have an enterprise-scale program, that's the case where a large consultancy earns its rate.

### What a specialist actually builds for you

One level deeper, and this is the part to hand off rather than run yourself. The foundation is a proper data model under your reports: your core numbers defined once as measures so revenue means one thing everywhere, clean structure linking each fact to date, product, and region, row-level security so each manager sees only their slice, and a scheduled refresh so the number on screen is always current. That's the heavy work a seasoned bi consultant does quickly because they've built it before. Once it's in place, keeping it running is light. Most of what a big engagement charges you for is the scaffolding around that core, not the core itself.

Frequently asked questions

What does business intelligence and consulting actually include? It covers the outside help that turns raw data into reports your team trusts. In practice that means building a clean data model, defining your core numbers once, setting up security and refresh, and delivering dashboards people actually use. The goal is one agreed number the business can act on, not just a pretty screen.

Is a specialist firm cheaper than a big consultancy? Usually yes, for a mid-sized job. A big firm's rate carries brand and overhead, and much of the build gets done by junior staff learning on your project. A specialist has fewer layers, so more of your money goes to the actual work, and they finish in weeks because they've done the same job many times.

When should we still hire a big consultancy? When the job is enterprise-scale, spans many countries and systems, and runs for years, or when a board or regulator insists on a recognized brand for the engagement. For a reporting problem that's really about a model that was never built right and a slow close, that scale is more cost than benefit.

What's the risk with power bi consulting services from a large firm? The main one is the last mile. Big engagements start and document well but can stall at the point where daily users take over. When the team rotates off, the knowledge can leave too, and you're back to silos. Ask upfront who builds it, who maintains it, and what you're left with when they go.

How fast can a specialist fix our reporting? For a mid-sized company, the core model is often weeks, sometimes less. Speed is the main thing you're paying a specialist for. They've built the same foundation many times, so they skip the long ramp a rotating team needs to learn your business first.

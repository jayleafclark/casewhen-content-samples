Natural Language Query Power BI: What Controllers Need to Know

Quick Answer: Natural language query Power BI features let you type a plain-English question such as "sales by region last quarter" and get a chart back, through the Q&A visual and now Copilot. It works well when your data model is clean and named clearly. When your model is messy or the numbers live in three places, it returns confident answers that are wrong, so the honest first step is fixing the model, not turning the feature on.

Your team wants to ask a question about the numbers and get an answer, without waiting three days for someone to build a report. That's the promise of natural language query Power BI, and it's a fair thing to want. You type "revenue by product this year" and a chart appears. No DAX to write, no ticket to file, no meeting to book. For a controller who fields the same five questions every close, that would be a genuine relief.

Here's the part nobody puts on the sales page. The feature answers whatever you ask, whether or not your model can back it up. Ask it for "margin" when your model has three different margin calculations and no agreed definition, and it hands you a number anyway. The number looks clean and finished, the reader acts on it, and it happens to be wrong.

What's natural language query Power BI?

Natural language query Power BI means you ask a question in everyday words and Power BI turns it into a chart or a number. There are two ways to do it today.

The first is the Q&A visual, which has been in Power BI for years. You drop it onto a report, and readers type questions such as "total sales by month" into a box. Power BI reads the field names in your model and builds the answer.

The second is Copilot in Power BI, the newer AI layer. Copilot goes further. It can write the DAX, summarize a report in a sentence, and answer broader questions across a whole semantic model. This is the one everyone is excited about, and it's the one that exposes a weak data model fastest.

Both options read your model. Neither of them fixes it.

Why does it give wrong answers?

Because the tool trusts your model completely, and most models aren't built to be trusted.

Say a reader types "show me net revenue." If your model has a field called `Revenue`, another called `Net Rev`, and a third called `Revenue_Final` from an old import, the tool picks one. It can't tell which field is correct, because nobody told it. You get an answer that looks finished and ties out to nothing.

The same thing breaks down with time. Ask for "this quarter" when your date table is incomplete, and the filter lands on the wrong range. The chart still renders and still looks polished, which is exactly the danger. A wrong number arrives looking as finished as a right one.

This is where the AI question turns into a data question. The AI feature depends entirely on the data underneath it. Precisely and Drexel LeBow found in their 2025 Data Integrity report that only 12% of organizations say their data is good enough in quality and access to use for AI. Natural language query Power BI sits right on top of that gap. Turn it on over an unready model and it distributes wrong answers faster than before.

What does a model need before you turn it on?

A model that answers plain-English questions correctly has a few things in place. None of them are exotic. All of them are work.

  • One agreed definition per number. One field for net revenue, named clearly, with the old duplicates removed. If your team can't agree what "margin" means, the tool can't either.
  • Clear field names. `Total Sales`, not `col_amt_3`. The tool reads names to understand questions, so the names have to be human.
  • A proper date table. So "last quarter" and "year to date" resolve to the right range every time.
  • Synonyms set up. If people say "turnover" and your field is called "Revenue," you add that synonym so the question maps to the right field.
  • Row-level security in place. So a regional manager asking "show me all deals" only sees their own region, not the whole company.

Does Copilot change the answer?

Copilot lifts both the capability and the risk together. It can do more, so it can be wrong in more places. It writes DAX you didn't check. It summarizes a report in plain language, and if the underlying measure is off, the summary is off too, in a confident and readable sentence that a busy executive forwards without checking.

Microsoft co-funds Copilot enablement, and there's real value in it. But that value only shows up on a governed, single-source model. On a messy one, Copilot becomes a faster way to hand out numbers you can't defend. The honest message stays plain. You can't just switch Copilot on. Your data has to be ready for it first.

FAQ

Is natural language query Power BI free? The Q&A visual is included in Power BI at no extra cost. Copilot in Power BI needs a Fabric or Premium capacity, so it carries a licensing cost on top of your standard Power BI plan.

Why does the Q&A visual give me the wrong number? Almost always because your model has more than one field for the same thing, unclear field names, or an incomplete date table. The tool picks a field and answers confidently. Fixing the model to one agreed definition per number solves it.

Do I need Copilot to ask questions in plain English? No. The Q&A visual has let readers type plain-English questions for years and is included in Power BI. Copilot adds broader questions, DAX generation, and summaries, though both still depend on a clean, governed model to give correct answers.

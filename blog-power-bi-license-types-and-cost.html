Power BI License Types and Cost: No Sales Pitch, Just the Math

Quick answer. Power BI license types and cost in 2026 break down into four options. Power BI Desktop is free. Power BI Pro is $14 per user each month. Premium Per User is $24 per user each month. Fabric capacity starts around $5,000 per month reserved and lets viewers read reports without a paid license. ---

Most teams get Power BI license types and cost wrong, and it shows up on the invoice. Someone in finance signs up 40 people for Pro because that's the button they saw first, and half of them only ever open one report a month. That's real money going out the door for seats nobody uses the way they think.

So before you sign anything, here's the honest version of Power BI license types and cost in 2026. No pitch. Just what each option is, what it costs, and who actually needs it. A lot of teams land here after trying to run reporting on spreadsheets, and that instinct is sound: 94% of business spreadsheets used in decision-making contain errors (Poon et al., 2024). Power BI fixes that, but only if you pay for the right shape of license.

Why is Power BI license pricing so confusing right now?

The confusion is real, and it isn't your fault. Microsoft changed the names.

For years the top tier was "Power BI Premium" sold as P SKUs (capacity you rented for the whole organization). Microsoft retired those P SKUs for new customers and folded that capacity into Microsoft Fabric, which uses F SKUs instead. So half the pages you find online still describe a product you can't buy anymore. That's the main reason the cost of Power BI license research feels like it contradicts itself.

On top of that, Microsoft raised prices on April 1, 2025. Pro went from $9.99 to $14 per user each month. Premium Per User went from $20 to $24. If an old blog quotes $9.99, it's out of date.

There are really only three ideas you need to keep in mind: who builds reports, who reads them, and how big your data is. Every license question comes back to those three.

A few things worth saying plainly about that table.

Power BI Desktop is free forever. You can build a full report on it and never pay a cent, as long as you keep it on your own machine. The moment you want to publish it to the cloud and let a coworker open it, you need Pro.

Power BI Pro is the workhorse. For most companies, the Power BI Pro license cost of $14 per user each month covers both building and sharing. Every person who publishes a report needs it, and (this is the part people miss) every person who opens that report also needs it, unless you're on Fabric capacity. We'll get to that.

Premium Per User is Pro with a bigger engine. You pay $24 instead of $14 and you get larger data models (up to 100 GB), refreshes as often as every 15 minutes, and paginated reports. It's a per-person upgrade, not a capacity you buy for everyone.

How do you pick the right license without overpaying?

Do this in order. It takes about ten minutes and saves the most money.

  1. Count your authors. These are the people who actually build and publish reports. Usually it's a handful, not the whole company. Every author needs at least Pro at $14 per user each month.
  2. Count your readers. These are people who only open reports and click around. Write down the real number. This is the group that decides whether you stay on Pro or move to Fabric capacity.
  3. Check your data size and refresh needs. If any author is fighting a model bigger than 1 GB, or needs data refreshed more than 8 times a day, that person needs Premium Per User at $24. Don't buy PPU for people who don't hit those limits.
  4. Do the reader math. If your readers at $14 each per month are climbing past roughly $5,000, price out Fabric capacity instead (next section). Below that line, Pro seats are almost always cheaper and simpler.
  5. Buy annual, not monthly, once you're settled. Microsoft charges the same headline price, but annual commitments avoid the churn of adding and removing seats every week.

The reason this order matters: the cost of Power BI license spend is driven almost entirely by readers, not authors. Ten authors on Pro is $140 a month. Four hundred readers on Pro is $5,600 a month. That second number is the one that decides your architecture.

When does Fabric capacity beat buying more Pro licenses?

This is the single biggest lever in Power BI license types and cost, so it's worth the numbers.

Fabric capacity F64 (the entry point that unlocks free viewing) costs about $5,002.67 per month on a one-year reservation, or about $8,409.60 per month pay-as-you-go. On F64 or higher, anyone who only reads reports does not need a paid seat. Your authors still need Pro, but your readers view for free.

So the break-even is simple. F64 reserved is roughly the price of 357 Pro seats (that is $5,002.67 divided by $14). If you have more than about 350 readers, Fabric capacity is cheaper than giving each of them a Pro license. If you have 40 readers, it's far more expensive, and Pro seats win by a mile.

Fabric capacity also gives you more than a licensing trick. It bundles data warehousing, pipelines, and OneLake storage into the same bill. For a large read audience that's growing, that combined price often makes sense before you even hit 350 readers, because you were going to buy some of those pieces anyway.

What does a Power BI Pro licence cost you beyond the sticker price?

The $14 is the easy part. The Power BI Pro licence cost that surprises people is the admin work around it.

Every reader needs a seat, so someone has to add and remove people as staff change. Forget to remove someone and you keep paying. Forget to add someone and they can't open Monday's report. This is boring, and it's exactly where money leaks.

There are two ways to keep the sticker price honest. First, use security groups in Microsoft Entra ID (formerly Azure AD) to assign licenses to groups, not individuals, so a new hire in the sales group gets a Pro seat automatically. Second, review usage. Power BI's usage metrics show you who actually opened a report in the last 30 days. If someone hasn't opened anything in three months, they probably don't need a paid seat.

For context on why this scale matters: Power BI is used by 375,000-plus organizations with 30-plus million monthly active users (Microsoft figures, 2025). Most of them started with a few Pro seats and only later discovered half their spend was on readers who never logged in. You can skip that stage by counting readers first.

Frequently asked questions

Is Power BI Desktop really free? Yes. Power BI Desktop is free to download and use forever for building reports on a Windows machine. You only start paying when you publish to the cloud and share with others, which needs a Pro license.

Do people who only view reports need a paid license? On Power BI Pro, yes. Every reader needs their own $14 per month seat. On Fabric capacity F64 or higher, readers view for free and only your authors need Pro.

What's the difference between Pro and Premium Per User? Pro at $14 covers building and sharing for normal-sized data. Premium Per User at $24 adds bigger models (up to 100 GB), refreshes as often as every 15 minutes, and paginated reports. PPU is a per-person upgrade, not capacity for the whole team.

Did Power BI prices go up? Yes. On April 1, 2025, Microsoft raised Pro from $9.99 to $14 per user each month and Premium Per User from $20 to $24. Any older figure you see is out of date.

What happened to Power BI Premium capacity (the P SKUs)? Microsoft retired the P SKUs for new customers and moved that capacity into Microsoft Fabric, sold as F SKUs. F64 is the entry point that gives you free report viewing for readers.

How do I know if Fabric capacity is worth it? Count your readers. If you have more than about 350 people who only view reports, Fabric F64 reserved (around $5,003 per month) is cheaper than buying them all Pro seats. Below that, Pro seats win.

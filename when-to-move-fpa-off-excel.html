When to move finance off Excel (and what waiting is costing you)

Quick answer. When to move finance off Excel comes down to three signals. Reconciliation and rework eat more hours every close. More than one person needs the same file at once. And the numbers in your management report stop matching each other. When any two of those show up together, a spreadsheet is no longer the right home for FP&A.

If you're asking when to move finance off Excel, part of you already knows the answer is close. You feel it every month. The last three days before the leadership review disappear into copy, paste, and check. One person owns the master file, and if they're on leave the close slips. Two reports quote the same revenue and land on two different numbers. None of that's a spreadsheet problem you can fix with one more tab.

This piece is for the controller or CFO who keeps patching the model and keeps ending up in the same spot. We'll name the pain, explain why it happens in plain terms, describe what good looks like, and give you a short list of what to ask for and what to fix first.

How do you know when to move finance off Excel?

Here's the honest test. Add up the hours your team spends each month getting to a number, versus the hours they spend deciding what to do about it. When the first number is bigger than the second, the tool is now working against you.

A few concrete signs, in the order they usually show up:

  • The close is slower than it was a year ago, and manual steps are the reason, even though the business hasn't gotten more complex.
  • The same file gets emailed around, and people work on different copies at the same time.
  • Someone asks "which version is current?" in a finance meeting, and nobody's sure.
  • A number on slide 3 doesn't match the same number on slide 9, and you find out in the room.

One of these is normal. Two of these at once is a signal. Three is a standing risk to the numbers your leadership team trusts.

Why does finance stay on Excel so long?

Because Excel is good, and because leaving feels expensive. The model works. Everyone knows it. It's been refined for years, and it carries logic that sits in one person's head. Replacing it reads as a project with no clear end, so it gets pushed to next quarter, every quarter.

There's also a quieter reason. The pain is spread out. Ten minutes of reconciliation here, an hour of version-checking there, a late night before the management report. No single moment is bad enough to force a decision, so the total cost never lands on one page where a CFO can see it. That's exactly why manual reconciliation and rework get underestimated. They scale with the business, and nobody's counting them.

What does staying on Excel cost you?

Start with error rate. In peer-reviewed research, 94% of business spreadsheets used in decision making contained errors (Panko et al., 2024). Most are small. Some move a number enough to change a call. When your reporting runs on spreadsheets, you're carrying that risk into every meeting whether you see it or not.

Then there's trust. Only 40% of executives say they always trust their organization's data (Talend). If the person presenting isn't fully sure the number is right, the room can feel it, and the meeting turns into a debate about the data instead of a decision about the business.

The dollar figure is real too. Gartner puts the average cost of poor data quality at $12.9 million a year. You won't recognize it as a line item. It shows up as slow closes, reruns, and decisions made on a stale copy.

And the calendar cost is measurable. High performers finish month-end in under three days. Eight days is common. If your close runs long and the reason is manual work, that gap is time your finance team isn't spending on the questions the business actually needs answered.

What are the signs to move FP&A off Excel?

The signs to move FP&A off Excel are the ones you can point at without a debate. Group them into three buckets, because two buckets lighting up at once is the real trigger.

Scale. The model has grown past what one file can carry cleanly. Refreshes take longer. Formulas break when someone inserts a row. The workbook opens slowly, and the person who knows why is the only person who knows why.

Concurrency. More than one person needs the same numbers at the same time, and Excel can't give them that without copies. Every copy is a new version of the truth, and 82% of companies say data silos disrupt their workflows (ZoomInfo). Your silos are the copies.

Reconciliation. The cost of manual reconciliation keeps climbing, and you're paying it in your best people's hours. When your senior analysts spend more of the month tying numbers together than explaining them, the tool has become the job.

What does good look like after you move?

Good is quiet. The close finishes on the same day every month because the manual steps are gone. When a number changes, it changes in one place, and every report that uses it updates. There's one definition of revenue, one of margin, one of churn, and people stop arguing about whose version is right.

Your team spends its month on analysis, not assembly. The management report ties out because it reads from the same source as everything else, so you're not checking it line by line the night before. And when a tool like Copilot answers a finance question, it returns the same number your team would, because the model underneath is defined and governed.

That last part matters more every quarter. A shared, well-built model is what lets you trust an automated answer. Without it, every new tool just adds another number to reconcile.

What should you fix first?

Don't rebuild everything at once. That's how these projects stall. Fix in this order.

  1. Pin the definitions. Agree, in writing, what revenue, margin, and your core measures mean. This is cheap and it removes most "whose number is right" arguments on its own.
  2. Pick the one report that hurts most. Usually it's the management report or the monthly close. Move that first, so the value is visible fast.
  3. Build the model once, properly. Get the data into a shared, structured model with clear ownership, so reconciliation runs on refresh instead of by hand.
  4. Govern access. Decide who sees what, and make one number the number. This is where trust comes from.

You don't need a giant platform on day one. You need one report moved off the spreadsheet and onto a source your team believes. The rest follows.

Frequently asked questions

Is Excel bad for finance? No. Excel is excellent for quick models and personal analysis. The problem starts when the numbers your whole company depends on live in a file only one person fully understands, and other people work off copies.

What do we move finance to? Most mid-sized companies move to a modeled BI setup on Power BI or Microsoft Fabric, sitting on a shared data source. The point isn't the logo. It's a single, governed model that returns one number and updates everywhere at once.

How long does it take to move FP&A off Excel? The first painful report can move in weeks, not months, if you scope it to one thing. A full move is a series of small steps, each one delivering a report your team trusts before you touch the next.

Won't we lose the logic built into our spreadsheets? No. The logic gets rebuilt in a defined model where it's documented and owned, instead of living in formulas one person maintains. You keep the thinking and lose the single point of risk.

How do we know the new numbers are right? You reconcile the new model against the spreadsheet during the move, number by number, until they match. After that, the model becomes the source and the checking stops being a monthly job.

What does it cost? Less than a slow close and a management report you can't fully trust. The bigger cost is usually waiting, because manual reconciliation and rework grow every month you stay.

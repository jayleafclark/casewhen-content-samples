Finance team spends too much time fixing data, and the fix is upstream

Quick answer: A finance team spends too much time fixing data when the numbers show up wrong from the source apps, so people spend the close copying, matching, and correcting instead of explaining what happened. The fix sits upstream, in one owned and defined set of numbers, not in another round of spreadsheet cleanup. ---

Ask most controllers where the month goes and you'll hear the same answer. The first week gets spent chasing numbers that don't agree, and the analysis leadership wants gets squeezed into the last hour before the deadline. When a finance team spends too much time fixing data, the job turns into cleanup work, and the part you were hired for gets crowded out.

This isn't a tooling problem you can buy your way out of. You already bought the tools. The pain shows up in three places every finance leader knows: a close that runs eight or ten days, a management report where the cash figure on one slide doesn't match the balance sheet on another, and a decision made in a meeting on a number that turned out to be a week old.

Why a finance team spends too much time fixing data

The numbers arrive wrong because the problem starts before finance ever opens the file. The upstream apps export slightly different versions of the same figure. The billing tool, the CRM, and the ledger each define revenue a little differently. No single one of them is the agreed source, so someone in finance has to line them up by hand every month. 82% of companies say data silos disrupt their workflows (zoominfo), and in finance that disruption has a name: manual data reconciliation. You match one export against another by hand because nothing upstream guarantees they'll agree.

The second reason is the spreadsheet. 94% of business spreadsheets used in decision-making contain errors (Poon et al., 2024). When the correction work lives in a chain of linked workbooks that one person maintains, the close depends on that person and their formulas being right. Miss a row, and a wrong number reaches leadership.

What's the data cleanup costing you at close and in the leadership review?

The hours are real, but the bigger cost is what they push out. When the first week of every month goes to fixing data, the close stretches to eight or ten days, and the analysis that would change a decision never gets written. A slow close is a slow answer to a real question: can we afford this hire, is this region slipping, did the price change work.

Then there's trust. Only 40% of executives always trust their data (Talend). So even when finance ships the number, leadership discounts it, and the meeting turns into an argument about whose figure is right instead of what to do next. Poor data quality costs organizations an average of 12.9 million dollars a year (Gartner), and a lot of that's decisions made slowly or made on the wrong number.

How long should a month-end close really take?

Here's a benchmark worth keeping. A month-end close of about eight days is common, and the strongest finance teams close in under three (solvexia). If yours runs longer, the extra days are almost always cleanup, not analysis. The teams that close fast didn't hire more people. They stopped finance from being the place data gets fixed and moved that fix upstream.

What does good look like when the numbers just work?

Good looks boring. The numbers show up the same in every report because they come from one agreed model, a single source of truth that finance, sales, and leadership all read from. No one reconciles by hand, because the reconciliation happens once inside the model instead of every month in a spreadsheet.

The shift isn't a bigger team or a nicer dashboard. It's where the correction happens. Once it happens upstream, the finance team goes back to reading the numbers instead of repairing them.

What should finance ask for, and fix first?

You don't need to rebuild everything at once. Ask three plain questions about your top three reports:

  • Where does each number come from, and is that source the agreed one?
  • Who owns the definition of revenue, margin, and cash, and is it written down once?
  • What in the close is a person fixing by hand that a model could fix once?

The first thing to fix is almost always the definition. When revenue means one thing in one place, most of the reconciliation work disappears, because there's nothing left to reconcile. That's the upstream fix, and it's the one that frees the team.

What your team does about it upstream

This is the one place it helps to go a level deeper, so here's the short version. The upstream fix is a single semantic model: one place where each number is defined once, in code, and every report reads from it. Sales, finance, and leadership pull the same revenue figure because there's only one. Access is set by role, so people see what they should. Refreshes run on a schedule you can trust, so a report is never a week old without anyone noticing.

None of that's a bigger finance team. It's moving the correction out of the month-end scramble and into the model, where it's done once and stays done. This is the work we do for finance teams, and it's usually faster to set up than one more quarter of manual cleanup.

Frequently asked questions

Why does finance spend more time fixing data than analysing it? Because the numbers arrive wrong from the upstream apps, so someone has to match and correct them before any analysis can start. The cause is upstream, in how the data is defined and owned, not in finance working too slowly.

Will new software fix this on its own? No. Most teams already own Power BI or a similar tool and still fix data by hand. The tool helps only once there's one agreed model behind it. Without that, a new tool gives you a faster way to build the same conflicting reports.

How fast can a month-end close realistically get? About eight days is common, and under three is the mark of a strong team (solvexia). Getting there's mostly about moving cleanup out of the close, not adding staff.

What's a single source of truth in plain terms? It's one model where each number is defined once, so every report and every person reads the same figure. When it exists, two reports can't disagree, because they're pulling from the same place.

Do we need a big project to start? No. Start with the definitions for your top three numbers and one owned model behind them. That single step removes most of the manual data reconciliation, and you can add the rest over time.

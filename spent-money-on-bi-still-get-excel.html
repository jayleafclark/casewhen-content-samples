You invested in BI but still get Excel exports. Here's what went wrong

Quick answer. If you invested in BI but still get Excel exports, the tool got installed on top of messy data instead of one shared model. So your team rebuilds the numbers by hand in spreadsheets, and two reports end up disagreeing. The fix is one agreed model that everyone reads from, not another dashboard. ---

You invested in BI but still get Excel exports, and you can watch it happen in any Monday meeting. Sales pulls a revenue number off one report. Finance has a different number in a spreadsheet somebody built the night before. The next twenty minutes go to arguing about which one is right, instead of deciding what to do about it. You paid for Power BI, you hired or trained analysts, and the number that matters still lives in a workbook that one person emails around.

This is one of the most common things owners and operators call us about. The tools are fine. The reports look modern. And the real work still happens in Excel, where nobody can see how a number was put together. Below is why that keeps happening, what a working setup looks like from your seat, and the few things to ask your team for first.

So you invested in BI but still get Excel exports?

Here is the pattern almost every time. Someone bought Power BI to replace the spreadsheets, but the data underneath never got cleaned up or agreed on. So the dashboard sits on top of the same mess, and the moment a number looks off, an analyst exports it to Excel to check it by hand. Once the answer lives in Excel, that is the version that gets emailed to you.

Three things usually drive it:

  • No agreed definition. Sales counts revenue when the deal is signed. Finance counts it when the invoice is paid. Both are honest. They will never match on a dashboard that was never told which one to use.
  • The dashboard shows numbers but not the workings. People trust a spreadsheet because they can click a cell and trace it. A report that just shows a total, with no way to see how it got there, gets exported so someone can pull it apart.
  • The report is hard to slice. If a manager can't filter down to their own region or their own product, they export the whole thing and cut it themselves. That is the export habit, and it never goes away on its own.

The analysts aren't the problem here. The setup keeps pushing them back to Excel, because the shared model they need was never built.

What is the Excel habit costing you?

More than the licences, and it shows up in places that hurt.

The clearest one is the leadership review. When cash on one slide doesn't reconcile with the number on another, your credibility takes the hit, not the spreadsheet's. Only 40% of executives say they always trust their organization's data (Talend). When your own pack has two answers for the same figure, you are living inside that statistic.

The second cost is time. Month-end drags because reconciliation is done by hand every cycle. Under three days is what high performers hit; eight is what most companies accept (Solvexia). If your close runs longer than that, it's usually because someone is stitching exports together by hand every cycle. The setup around the tool is what needs fixing.

The third cost is the decision itself. Manual spreadsheets carry mistakes far more often than anyone assumes. A 2024 peer-reviewed study by Poon and colleagues found errors in 94% of business spreadsheets used for decisions. When a real choice, a hire, a price change, a cut, rides on a hand-built export, the odds that one cell is off are not small. Poor data quality already costs organizations $12.9M a year on average (Gartner), and most of that leaks out through exactly these small, invisible mistakes.

Why do reports show different numbers?

Because they were built to answer different questions, and nobody wrote down the one definition they should share.

Each spreadsheet in your company is its own little source of truth. One counts returns, one doesn't. One filters out internal test orders, one leaves them in. One is a week older than the other. Every copy is slightly different, so every copy gives a slightly different answer. This is why 82% of leaders say data silos disrupt their workflows (ZoomInfo). The numbers aren't wrong so much as unaligned, and there is no shared place to settle which is correct.

A dashboard doesn't fix this by itself. If you point Power BI at five spreadsheets that already disagree, you get a nicer-looking version of the same disagreement. The disagreement lives in the data, not the chart.

What does good BI look like?

From your seat, good is simple to describe. You ask for a number, you get one answer, and you can find out how it was worked out without opening a spreadsheet.

Underneath, that comes from one shared model. Revenue is defined once, in one place. Every report reads from that same definition, so sales, finance, and the management report all show the same figure by default. That is what a single source of truth means in plain terms: one agreed set of rules for what each number is and where it comes from, read by every report. It can sit in a compact model that fits in your reporting tool.

When that exists, the export habit dies on its own. Nobody pulls a report into Excel to double-check it, because the report is already the trusted copy. Your analysts spend their time answering questions instead of reconciling. Around 70% of dashboard work is currently wasted because people don't trust or use what gets built (RevealBI). A trusted model is what turns that number around.

What should you ask your team to fix first?

You don't need to learn Power BI to fix this. You need to ask for the right things and know a good answer when you hear one.

  1. Ask for one owned model of your core numbers. Revenue, margin, and cash, defined once, with a named owner for each. If nobody can tell you who owns the definition of revenue, that is the first thing to fix.
  2. Ask where each number is defined. The answer should be one place, not "it depends which report." If it depends, you don't have a single source of truth yet.
  3. Ask for row-level security. This lets each manager see their own slice inside the same report, so they stop keeping private copies. It is the quiet reason exports multiply.
  4. Ask for scheduled refresh. The report should update itself on a timetable, so the current number is always the one on screen, and nobody exports last week's data by accident.

### What your team does about it

One level deeper, and this is the part to hand off rather than run yourself. The fix is a proper data model sitting under the reports: a clean structure where each business fact connects to things like date, product, and region, with the core numbers written as measures once. Your team builds that model, moves the shared definitions into it, turns on row-level security, and sets the refresh. From then on, every report reads the same numbers. This is real work, and it is the work that makes the tool you already bought finally pay off. It is also the work most teams don't have spare time to do while running the monthly close, which is usually when an outside pair of hands helps.

Frequently asked questions

Why do our two dashboards show different revenue? Because each one reads from a different source with a different definition. One may count signed deals, the other paid invoices; one may include returns, the other not. Until revenue is defined once in a shared model, both reports keep giving honest but different answers.

We already have Power BI. Why does finance still live in Excel? Usually because the model underneath was never built or agreed on. The dashboard shows totals, but people can't trace them, so they export to Excel to check the workings by hand. Fix the model and the trust moves to the report.

What is a single source of truth in plain terms? It means every report reads the same definitions for your core numbers, so a figure gets worked out one way and one way only. When it exists, sales, finance, and the management report all show the same figure without anyone reconciling by hand.

How long should month-end close take? High performers close in under three days; around eight is what most companies accept (Solvexia). If yours runs longer, it's usually the manual setup around the tool, where someone stitches exports together each cycle, that adds the extra days.

Do we need to replace Power BI to fix this? Almost never. The reports are usually fine. What's missing is the shared model and the definitions underneath them. That is a build on top of what you have, and it makes the software you already paid for start earning its keep.

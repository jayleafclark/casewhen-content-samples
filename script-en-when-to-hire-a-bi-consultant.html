# When to Hire a Power BI Consultant: 5 Signs It's Time (and Consultant vs In-House)

Host: Austin · Cluster: buyer · Target: 11 min · Primary keyword: when to hire a power bi consultant

Script

[0:00-0:12] HOOK (MODALITY: screen-record 3-second payoff demo, then hard cut to talking head) (ON-SCREEN: keyword card "When to hire a Power BI consultant")

Your numbers are wrong, your refresh takes forever, and half your team is back in Excel. At some point that stops being a tooling problem and starts being a hiring question.

[0:12-0:45] PROMISE + STAKES + OPEN LOOP (MODALITY: talking head, then b-roll of a Berlin office and hands on a keyboard) (ON-SCREEN: "The signs + who to call")

By the end of this you'll know the exact signs that mean it's time to bring in outside help, and you'll know whether to put someone on your payroll or bring in a firm. I run a Power BI and Fabric consultancy in Berlin, so I sit on the other end of this call most weeks. I'm going to give you the whole decision, free, so you can make it without me. And near the end I'll hand you the one question that tells you which way to go, so you don't gamble a year of budget on the wrong hire.

[0:45-2:05] VALUE BEAT 1 (MODALITY: screen-record of a refresh log, then data-viz of refresh time climbing) (ON-SCREEN card: "Sign one: reports show up late, and you don't trust them when they do")

CLAIM: The first sign is speed and trust at the same time.

PROOF: Your report takes thirty seconds to load. Your overnight refresh runs long, or it stops halfway and nobody notices until the Monday meeting. So the board pack goes out at eleven at night, and you're reading a number you're already unsure about. Here's what that looks like from the inside. This is a refresh log on a real setup. Watch the clock. This one runs forty minutes, and it's touching every row of history every single night, even though only yesterday changed.

PAYOFF (ON-SCREEN takeaway): If your refresh time keeps climbing and your close keeps slipping, that's sign one. Solvexia's benchmark is a close inside eight days, and the strong teams do it in under three. If you're past ten days and getting slower, the setup has outgrown whoever built it.

RE-HOOK: Slow is annoying. The next sign is the one that costs you a decision in the room.

[2:05-3:25] VALUE BEAT 2 (MODALITY: custom animation, then on-screen stat) (ON-SCREEN card: "Sign two: two reports, same metric, two different answers")

CLAIM: Sign two shows up in a meeting.

PROOF (custom animation, described): two dashboards side by side, both labelled "Revenue", showing two different figures. A magnifying glass slides over each one and reveals that each pulls from a different definition, with no shared source in the middle. As the voiceover lands, the two definitions light up in the CaseWhen brand green. Sales pulls revenue from one report. Finance pulls it from another. The two numbers don't match, and the next twenty minutes go to arguing about whose spreadsheet is right instead of what to do. The reason is almost always the same: two reports, two different definitions of the same word, and no single place that says which one is correct. That disagreement has a real cost. Top finance teams close the month in five days or fewer, and the slowest take ten or more, according to APQC in 2023. So if the room doesn't believe the screen, you're in good company, and it's fixable.

PAYOFF (ON-SCREEN takeaway): If people keep their own version in Excel because they don't trust the official report, that's sign two. It means you don't have one agreed number yet.

RE-HOOK: Which leads straight into the third sign, the one you can spot just by walking the floor.

[3:25-4:45] VALUE BEAT 3 (MODALITY: b-roll of someone exporting a report to Excel on a laptop, then kinetic type) (ON-SCREEN card: "Sign three: you bought the tools, and everyone still lives in Excel")

CLAIM: Sign three is the one you already feel.

PROOF: You paid for Power BI. You maybe paid for Fabric on top. And your team still exports every report to Excel to get their actual work done. That's a design problem, not a discipline one. When reports don't answer the questions people ask, people rebuild them by hand. And there's a hidden risk here. Often one person understands the master spreadsheet. If they're on holiday, the close stalls. Zoominfo found eighty-two percent of teams say data silos disrupt their work. Excel sprawl is just silos you can see.

PAYOFF (ON-SCREEN takeaway): If the tool you bought didn't replace the spreadsheets, and everything still runs through one person's laptop, that's sign three.

RE-HOOK: Three signs. Now the part most owners skip: what a proper fix looks like, one level down.

[4:45-6:15] VALUE BEAT 4 (MODALITY: custom animation of a star schema and layered data model, then screen-record before/after refresh time) (ON-SCREEN card: "What a real fix looks like under the hood, the one technical beat")

CLAIM: I'll go one level down here, just once, so you know what you're paying for. Then straight back to the business.

PROOF (custom animation, described): a messy pile of flat tables on the left reorganises into a star schema, one fact table in the centre with dimension tables snapping into place around it, one-to-many arrows lighting up. Then three layers appear, raw to cleaned to trusted, with data flowing left to right. When a firm like mine fixes this, it's usually four plain moves. One: one data model where revenue is defined once, so every report and every Copilot answer returns the same number. Two: the data gets shaped into a star schema, the layout that makes reports fast instead of slow. Three: row-level security, so each person opens the report and sees only their region or their accounts, from that same single source. Four: incremental refresh, so the overnight job only reloads yesterday, not ten years of history. That's the forty-minute refresh dropping to a couple of minutes, which you can see right here in the before and after.

PAYOFF (ON-SCREEN takeaway): You don't need to build this. You need to recognise it. If a hire or a firm can't explain those four in plain words, they can't fix your reporting.

RE-HOOK: So who does that work, someone on your payroll or a firm you bring in? Here's how to tell.

[6:15-8:00] VALUE BEAT 5 (MODALITY: data-viz two-column compare, then talking head) (ON-SCREEN card: "Consultant or in-house: which one fits your situation?")

CLAIM: Now the real decision. Should you hire someone onto your team, or bring in a firm?

PROOF: Both can be right. It depends on three things: how big the job is, how long it lasts, and whether you'll keep needing it. Hire in-house when the work is steady and permanent. If reporting is central to how you run every week, and you'll always have new dashboards and new questions, a full-time analyst pays for themselves. The downside is time and risk. A good Power BI hire in a market like Berlin is expensive and slow to find, and while you search, the numbers stay wrong. And if you hire one person, you've moved the single-person risk from a spreadsheet to a salary. Bring in a firm when you need it fixed now, or when you need senior skill you can't justify full-time. A firm has already built the thing you need fifty times over. They set the foundation in weeks, not quarters. The trade-off is they're not on your payroll forever, so you want them to leave you something your own people can run. Gartner puts the cost of poor data quality at an average of 12.9 million dollars a year for a business. Against a number like that, arguing over a day rate is the wrong argument.

PAYOFF (ON-SCREEN takeaway): Simple rule. Ongoing and central to the business, hire. Fix it once, fix it fast, or fix it right the first time, bring in a firm. Plenty of teams do both: a firm builds the foundation, then trains your person to run it.

RE-HOOK: Which brings me to the one question I promised, the one that de-risks the whole call.

[8:00-9:30] PAYOFF, OPEN LOOP + DE-RISK BEAT (MODALITY: talking head, then kinetic type of the question on screen) (ON-SCREEN card: "The one question that de-risks the hire")

Here's the question I promised. Ask any consultant, and any candidate, this: in ninety days, what will my team be able to do without you? A good answer is specific. One trusted revenue number. A refresh under five minutes. Your analyst trained to add new reports on their own. If the answer is vague, or if it sounds like you'll depend on them forever, treat that as your warning. That one question de-risks the decision, because it moves the conversation from selling to leaving you better off. It works on an internal hire too: what will you have built and documented in the first quarter? And a smaller de-risk move: don't open with a twelve-month contract. Start with one small paid piece of work. Score where you stand first, then scope one fix. If they can't turn one clear problem into one clear result, you've learned that cheaply.

[9:30-11:00] PAYOFF RECAP + SOFT CTA (MODALITY: kinetic type recap list, then talking head with link cards) (ON-SCREEN: 5-point recap, then two link cards)

Quick recap. Sign one: reports are slow and late, and you don't trust them. Sign two: two reports disagree on the same number. Sign three: you bought the tools and everyone still works in Excel. Any one of those on its own is fine. Two or three together is your setup telling you it's outgrown do-it-yourself. On consultant versus in-house: ongoing and central, hire; need it fixed now or need senior skill, bring in a firm; and either way, ask the ninety-day question. That question is the whole game.

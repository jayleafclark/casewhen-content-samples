Consultant Business Intelligence: The Questions to Ask Before You Sign

Quick answer. QUICK ANSWER

A consultant business intelligence engagement means hiring an outside specialist to plan, build, and hand over your reporting on Power BI, Microsoft Fabric, or Azure. A good one owns the data model, the security rules, and the refresh, then trains your team to run it. Judge them on scope, references, and a fixed first deliverable.

Most teams pick a consultant business intelligence partner on a gut call, and it shows up six months later in a Power BI report the finance team no longer opens.

The numbers on the board slide stop matching the numbers in the source ledger. Nobody can say which one is right. At that point you are paying twice: once for the build, and again for the meetings spent arguing about whose figure to believe. This is common. Across 550-plus professionals, 67% of organizations say they do not fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Precisely and Drexel LeBow, 2025). A good consultant business intelligence partner exists to close that trust gap, and a weak one widens it.

This guide walks through what the role covers, why so many builds stall, and the exact questions to ask before you sign a statement of work.

What does a consultant business intelligence hire really do?

The title covers a wide range, so pin down the work. At the strong end, a business intelligence consulting engagement owns four things:

  1. The data model. They shape your tables, relationships, and DAX measures into one semantic model that everyone reports from, so "revenue" means the same thing on every page.
  2. Security. They set up row-level security so a regional manager sees only their region, and they test it with View As before anyone gets access.
  3. Refresh and delivery. They wire up incremental refresh so a large fact table reloads only the last few days each morning instead of the whole history, and they schedule it to finish before your team logs in.
  4. Handover. They document the setup and train two of your people to keep it running after they leave.

A weaker hire builds a pretty dashboard, emails you a .pbix file, and disappears. Six months on, the one person who understood it has changed jobs, and nobody can change a filter without breaking a visual. Ask any Power BI consultant to describe their handover step before you talk about design. The answer tells you which end of that range they sit on.

Why do so many Power BI projects stall after go-live?

The build usually goes fine. The trouble starts the first month the data changes.

Here is the mechanism. A report gets built against last quarter's data with no plan for growth. Then a new product line, a renamed cost center, or an acquired company arrives, and the model has no room for it. The refresh that took two minutes in the demo now takes forty and times out. The single "sales" measure turns out to double-count returns because credit notes were never modeled. None of this appears in a two-week proof of concept. It only appears at scale, months later, when the person who built it is gone.

Good Power BI consulting plans for that day one. That means a dated fact table with RangeStart and RangeEnd parameters set up for incremental refresh, a star schema that new dimensions can slot into, and deployment pipelines that move a change from development to test to production without hand-editing the live report. These are standard Power BI features, so the gap is rarely the tooling. It comes down to whether the consultant built for month twelve or only for the demo.

How do you tell a real Power BI consultant from a resume?

Certifications and logos on a slide say little about whether someone can run your close on time. Use these five checks instead, and do them today:

  1. Ask for a live walkthrough of a model they built, with the DAX visible. If they show slides instead of Power BI Desktop, that answers the question.
  2. Ask how they handle a measure that has to ignore a filter. A strong answer names CALCULATE and REMOVEFILTERS and explains why. A vague answer means they lean on visuals and copied code.
  3. Ask what breaks their refresh most often, and what they do about it. Real practitioners have a fast, specific answer about credentials, gateways, or a slow source query.
  4. Ask for one reference where the client kept running the reports without the consultant a year later. This is the single hardest thing to fake.
  5. Give them a small paid test build, one report against your real data, before the full contract. A week of real work tells you more than three rounds of interviews.

None of these need a technical buyer to run. They need a willing consultant and a real dataset.

What should a business intelligence consulting scope actually spell out?

A loose statement of work is where budgets drift. A tight one names the deliverable, the data model, the owner of each dataset, and the training. Compare what a thin scope and a strong scope put in writing.

What to checkThin scope (risky)Strong scope (sign this)
Deliverable"A Power BI dashboard"Named reports, listed pages, and the exact measures each one shows
Data modelNot mentionedOne shared semantic model, star schema, documented relationships
Security"Access controls"Row-level security roles listed, tested with View As before go-live
Refresh"Scheduled refresh"Incremental refresh on named tables, finish time before 8am
Handover"Documentation"Two of your staff trained, a written runbook, 30 days of support
Change processNot mentionedDeployment pipelines from dev to test to production

If a proposal skips the right-hand column, ask for it in writing before you sign. A good Power BI consulting services provider will already have most of it. A weak one will treat every added line as extra cost.

How much does business intelligence consulting cost, and what drives it?

Rates vary by region and seniority, so anchor on drivers instead of a single figure. Independent senior Power BI consultants in Europe commonly bill between roughly 800 and 1,600 euros a day. A boutique consultancy runs higher per day and lower in total, because a specialist finishes in a fifth of the hours a generalist needs. The three things that move the bill:

  • Data quality at the source. If your source tables are messy, most of the budget goes into cleanup before a single chart appears. Poor data quality costs organizations 12.9 million dollars per year on average (Gartner), and cleanup you skip now you pay for later.
  • Number of sources. One clean SQL database is a short job. Six systems with different customer IDs is a data-modeling project.
  • Whether you buy a build or a capability. A one-off report is cheaper up front and stalls at go-live. A trained team costs more and keeps running.

On top of the consultant, budget the platform. A Power BI Pro license runs about 14 dollars per user per month, Premium Per User about 24 dollars, and Fabric capacity starts at a small F2 tier billed by the hour. A good consultant sizes that for you so you pay for what your team uses.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a BI consultant and a data engineer? A data engineer moves and cleans data into a warehouse. A consultant business intelligence specialist turns that data into reports people read and trust, and often does light engineering to get there. On a small team, one person does both.

Do I need a consultant if we already have Power BI licenses? Licenses give you the tool. A consultant gives you a model that stays correct as your data grows, security that stands up, and a team that can run it. The license is the cheap part.

How long does a first Power BI project take? A focused first report against clean data takes two to four weeks. If the data needs cleanup and modeling across several sources, plan for two to three months to a stable go-live.

Can one consultant cover Power BI, Fabric, and Azure? Some can, for a mid-sized setup. For a large migration, you want a small team with a data engineer and a report specialist. Ask who does which part before you sign.

What is the first thing a good consultant asks for? Access to your real data and one clear question the report must answer. If the first conversation is about visuals and colors, that is a warning sign.

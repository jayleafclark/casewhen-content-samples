BI Consulting Company: How to Actually Evaluate One

Quick answer. A BI consulting company builds the reporting your business runs on. A BI consulting company builds the reporting your business runs on and hands it over so your team trusts the numbers without phoning anyone. That's the whole job. And it matters more than the price tag suggests: 67% of organizations don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Drexel LeBow and Precisely, 2025), and Gartner puts the cost of that mess at $12.9M a year for the average organization. The choice you're making is bigger than the price tag: whether next quarter's board meeting argues about the numbers or acts on them.

If you're a controller, a CFO, or the person who owns reporting, this is the plain version for you. You'll see the same service called a bi consultancy, a consultant business intelligence firm, or plain business intelligence and consulting. Same work under every name. Here's what that work is, how to tell a strong firm from a risky one, what it costs, and how to decide before you sign.

What does a BI consulting company do?

A BI consulting company turns messy source data into reports your team believes. Strip away the marketing and it's four steps, always in this order.

First comes the data model. That means shaping your tables so a word like revenue means one thing in every report instead of three. Get this step wrong and every chart downstream inherits the error. Buyers rarely see this part, and it decides everything.

Second, the business logic gets written once. Margin, churn, rolling twelve-month sales, each defined in a single place so they can't drift apart. The day someone types a number straight into a slide is the day you learn it drifted.

Third comes refresh and access. Reports update on a schedule, big tables load quickly, and a regional manager sees only their region. This is what keeps the numbers current and private.

Fourth, and this is the step weak firms skip, the work gets handed back to you. A firm that can't teach your analyst to run the model in an afternoon has sold you a dependency, not reporting you own.

Most buyers judge the work on step three, the pretty dashboard. The value sits in steps one and four, and neither shows up in a demo.

How do you evaluate a BI consulting company?

A BI consulting company earns the contract on what survives the first hard month-end, not the demo. The demo always looks sharp. The invisible parts are where projects go sideways.

Here's the usual sequence. The report runs fine at demo size. Then the data grows, the nightly refresh starts timing out, and someone reruns it by hand every morning before the leadership meeting. A month-end close that should take a strong team a few days, and stretches to eight or ten for most (SolveXia), now hangs on one person and one fragile refresh.

Then trust cracks. The board pack shows one revenue figure and the finance export shows another, and the meeting turns into a fight about whose number is right. Only 40% of executives say they always trust their organization's data (Talend). A report nobody trusts gets re-checked against a spreadsheet, so you end up paying for two versions of the truth.

So judge the firm on the dull questions. Ask how the refresh behaves when the data is ten times bigger. Ask who owns the model after they walk out. Ask to watch the numbers reconcile on your data before launch. A firm that welcomes those questions is the one worth hiring.

What separates a good BI consulting company from a risky one?

Good work leaves you a short, boring checklist you can verify. A strong bi consultancy delivers every item below, and you should ask to see each one before you call a project done.

  • One definition per number, so every report that uses revenue agrees to the cent.
  • A refresh you can count on, so the report is ready when you open it at 8am.
  • Access set correctly, so each person sees their own numbers and nothing they shouldn't.
  • A record of who opens each report, so you can retire the ones nobody reads.
  • Written documentation and one training session, so your team can change a number without opening a ticket.

That last point pays off for years. 94% of business spreadsheets used in decision-making contain errors (Poon et al., 2024), and the reason you're hiring help is to move off fragile spreadsheets and onto numbers you can defend in a board meeting. You keep that result only if the trusted model stays with your team. Around 70% of dashboard work goes unused (RevealBI), almost always because the thing shipped but nobody could rely on it. A good firm shows you the model, explains the refresh in plain words, and proves access works before launch. Vagueness on any of these is the tell.

How much should a BI consulting company cost?

Rates move around, but the shape of the bill is predictable. You're choosing between a few ways to buy the same expertise, and each one carries a trap.

How you buyWhat you getBest fitWatch out for
Day rateAd-hoc fixes and small reportsA team that just needs extra handsScope creep with no fixed end
Fixed-scope projectA defined build with a documented handoverA first real dashboard or a migrationFuzzy deliverables and no handover clause
RetainerOngoing changes and supportReports that change oftenPaying monthly for work already done
Embedded consultantA senior person inside your team for monthsLong builds with knowledge transferThat person becoming your only expert

Software licensing sits under all of these and gets billed apart from the fee. On the Microsoft stack that's a per-user or capacity cost, and a firm that skips it on the first call will surprise you on the invoice. Ask for the licensing math up front so the total is clear before you commit.

The number that matters most isn't the day rate. It's what you own at the end. A cheaper build that only its author understands turns expensive the day that person is unreachable. Business intelligence and consulting work earns its fee on the handover, so pay for the part that stays with you, not just the build you can see.

Should you hire a BI consulting company or build in-house?

It comes down to how often the work changes and how much of it there is. Both paths work, and an honest firm tells you which one you need rather than selling you the bigger one.

Hire in-house when reporting is central to your business and changes every week, when there's enough work to keep a skilled person busy, and when you can pay to keep them. A full-time hire builds deep knowledge of your data and is there every day.

Bring in a firm when you need a strong build fast, when the heavy work is a one-time project such as a first dashboard or a migration, or when your team can maintain reports but can't design the foundation. 82% of companies say data silos disrupt their workflows (ZoomInfo), and untangling that kind of cross-team mess is exactly what an outside build is good at.

Most teams do both. A firm builds the foundation and trains the team, then the team runs it and calls for help on the next big project. What you're buying either way is a set of numbers everyone agrees on, so decide on who can build and keep that, not on the logo on the invoice.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a BI consulting company and a data agency? A bi consulting company focuses on the reporting layer: the model, the numbers, and the dashboards your team reads to decide. A broader data agency may also move and clean data upstream. On a small project one team does both. On a bigger one, ask which half each firm is strongest at, because a straight answer tells you plenty.

Do I need a BI consulting company if I already own Power BI? Owning the tool is the easy part. The reliable model, the trusted numbers, and the tested access are the real work, and that's what most teams bring help in for. A capable firm gets the first solid build and the handover right, then your team runs it from there.

How long does a first BI project take? A focused first dashboard with a clean model, a dependable refresh, and correct access usually runs four to eight weeks. Anyone promising a company-wide build you can trust in a week is promising a demo, not something you can rely on at month-end.

Should we hire a big firm or an independent consultant? Big firms give you process and bench depth. An independent usually puts the senior person on your project instead of a junior doing the build. For a first Power BI or Fabric project, senior hands-on time tends to matter more than the brand on the invoice.

What's the biggest mistake buyers make? Approving on the demo and skipping the handover talk. Ask how you'll run the reports after the firm leaves, and ask it on day one, in writing.

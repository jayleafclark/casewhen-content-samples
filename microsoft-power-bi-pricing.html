Microsoft Power BI Pricing: The Real Number Nobody Publishes

Microsoft Power BI pricing runs from $0 for personal use to $14 per user per month for Pro and $24 for Premium Per User. Bigger rollouts add capacity from about $5,000 a month. The published price is real, and it's also only part of what you pay: your true cost includes the build work that makes the numbers trustworthy. ---

Microsoft Power BI pricing starts at $0 for one person and $14 per user per month to build and share reports, but the number that lands on your invoice is usually a few times the sticker price once you add capacity and the work behind reports the board will actually believe.

That gap is the part vendors leave off the pricing page, and it's the part a controller or CFO gets asked about in the budget meeting. So here are the published numbers first, then the three things that decide what you really spend.

What does Microsoft Power BI pricing include?

There are four tiers, and most of the confusion comes from people comparing the wrong two. The per-user prices are what you see on the Microsoft site. Capacity is the big line item that shows up once you have a lot of people opening reports.

Here is the plain version.

TierPublished priceWhat it coversWho it's for
Free$0One person building for themselves, no proper sharingA single analyst testing the waters
Pro$14 per user per monthBuild, share, and open reports across a teamMost small and mid-sized teams
Premium Per User$24 per user per monthBigger models, more refreshes, paginated reports, some Fabric features, per personPower users and finance teams with heavy models
Premium capacity (Fabric)From about $5,000 per monthAny number of viewers open reports without each buying a seatLarger companies with hundreds of report readers

The first three are priced per person. The fourth is priced by the size of the engine you rent, not by headcount. Picking between them is the decision that moves your bill the most, so it's worth being clear on how the per-user side and the premium side compare.

How much does Power BI cost per user?

For most teams, the answer is $14 a month per person on Pro. Everyone who builds a report needs a Pro seat, and on standard Pro, everyone who opens one needs a Pro seat too. So a finance team of ten builders and forty viewers is fifty Pro seats, which is $700 a month before anything else.

That per-seat math is where the surprise usually hits. Companies budget for the ten people who build reports and forget the forty who just read them. Each reader is another $14, and a report nobody reads is still a report you paid to license. Around 70% of dashboard work goes unused (RevealBI), so a chunk of those viewer seats often sit on reports that were never adopted in the first place.

If your reader count climbs past a few hundred, the per-seat model stops making sense and capacity takes over. That's the moment premium enters the picture.

What is Power BI Premium pricing, and when is it worth it?

Power BI Premium pricing has two shapes, and they solve different problems.

The first is Premium Per User. The Power BI Premium cost here is $24 a month per person, and it's still a per-seat price. You pay it for the people who need bigger datasets, more frequent refreshes, or paginated reports for pixel-perfect financial statements. For a small group of heavy finance users, that's often the cleanest answer.

The second is Premium capacity, now sold as Microsoft Fabric capacity. This one starts around $5,000 a month for the entry tier, and it lets any number of people read reports without each buying a paid seat. That Power BI Premium price only pays off once your viewer count is high enough that buying everyone a Pro seat would cost more. As a rough line, somewhere north of about 350 to 500 readers, capacity gets cheaper than seats.

So the premium decision comes down to one question: are you paying for a few power users, or for a crowd of viewers? The first is Premium Per User at $24. The second is capacity in the thousands. Get this wrong and you either overpay for seats you didn't need or overpay for a capacity you couldn't fill.

Why is the sticker price never your real total?

Because the license is the cheap part. The expensive part is making the numbers in the report trustworthy, and that never shows up on a pricing page.

You can license Power BI on Friday and still be in the same budget meeting a month later, arguing about why the sales report and the finance report disagree on revenue. Only 40% of executives say they always trust their company's data (Talend), and a fresh license does nothing to change that. The tool renders whatever the model feeds it, and if the model is wrong, you've just paid to distribute a wrong number faster.

The real total has three parts stacked on top of each other:

  1. Licenses. The $14, $24, or capacity figure above. This is the only part vendors quote you.
  2. The build. Someone has to connect the sources, write the model so every report agrees on what revenue means, set row-level security, and schedule the refreshes. This is where a rushed setup costs you more later.
  3. The trust tax. If the model is shaky, you pay again every month in reconciliation, in duplicate reports, and in meetings spent arguing about whose number is right instead of deciding anything.

That third cost is the one that hurts, because it's invisible on the invoice and painful in the room. Poor data quality costs organizations an average of $12.9M a year (Gartner), and almost none of that is license spend. Roughly 94% of business spreadsheets used in decision-making contain at least one error (Poon et al., 2024), and a Power BI report built straight on top of those spreadsheets inherits every one of them.

How do you work out your actual Power BI bill?

Start with a headcount, not a license page. Write down two numbers: how many people will build reports, and how many will only read them.

Multiply builders by $14 for their Pro seats. For readers, run the same $14 per seat, then compare that against the roughly $5,000 a month capacity price. Whichever is smaller is your licensing floor. Add Premium Per User at $24 only for the specific power users who need the bigger models.

Then add the part that's easy to skip: the build. A single clean report on two or three connected sources is a few days of work. A proper finance model across many sources, with agreed definitions and security, is a few weeks. Budget for it once, or pay for it forever in manual cleanup.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Power BI cost for a small business? For a small team, Power BI Pro at $14 per user per month is the usual answer. A team of ten builders and readers is $140 a month. You only move off per-seat pricing once you have hundreds of readers, at which point rented capacity becomes the cheaper route.

Is Power BI free version enough for a company? No, not for a company. The free tier is built for one person working alone and can't share reports properly across a team. The moment two people need to see the same governed report, you need Pro seats at $14 each.

What is the difference between Power BI Pro and Premium? Pro is $14 per user per month and covers building and sharing for most teams. Premium comes in two forms: Premium Per User at $24 a month for heavy individual users, and Premium capacity from around $5,000 a month that covers a crowd of readers under one rented engine instead of per seat.

When does Power BI Premium capacity make sense? Once you have enough report readers that buying each one a Pro seat costs more than the capacity itself, usually somewhere past 350 to 500 viewers. Below that, per-seat Pro is almost always cheaper.

Does the license price include setup and support? No. The published Microsoft Power BI pricing covers the software only. Connecting your sources, building the model so reports agree on the numbers, and setting security are separate work, done in-house or by a partner. That build cost is usually larger than the first year of licenses.

Will Power BI pricing go up? It has before. Pro moved from $9.99 to $14 and Premium Per User from $20 to $24 in recent years. Treat today's numbers as a floor for budgeting and leave room for the per-user prices to rise again.

Power BI Pricing, Line by Line, With Real Numbers

Quick answer. Power BI pricing has three real numbers. It is free for one person, $14 per user a month for Power BI Pro so people can share reports, and $24 per user for Power BI Premium Per User. Go bigger and you pay for capacity instead, starting around $5,000 a month. The license is rarely the part that costs you. ---

You asked for Power BI pricing, got a quote back, and the number on it made the whole decision look like it comes down to which seat you buy. The per-seat number is the easy part. The cost that actually hurts sits on a line nobody put in front of you.

You run the company. You approve the spend. The quote in front of you has a per-user price that looks small and a capacity price that looks alarming, with no easy way to tell which one fits you. So the decision stalls, or someone signs for the big platform to be safe and you find out later that half of it sits unused.

You feel the real cost somewhere else. A leadership review where sales quotes one revenue figure and finance quotes another, and twenty minutes go to arguing about whose number is right. A month-end close that drags past a week because one person stitches the spreadsheets together by hand. A decision made on last quarter's figure because nobody was sure the report was current. No line on the price sheet fixes any of that.

What does Power BI pricing look like in plain numbers?

Here is the licensing, without the sales deck.

TierWhat it costsWhat you getWho it is for
Power BI (Free)FreeBuild and view your own reports, no sharing to othersOne analyst working alone
Power BI Pro$14 per user a monthPublish, share, and open shared reportsMost teams that consume dashboards
Power BI Premium Per User$24 per user a monthEverything in Pro plus larger models, more frequent refreshes, and paginated reportsHeavy report builders and finance
Capacity (Microsoft Fabric F-SKUs)From roughly $5,000 a monthA reserved slice of compute so viewers open reports without their own seatLarger rollouts with many viewers

Two things surprise buyers here. First, you may already own Power BI Pro. It ships inside some Microsoft 365 plans, so before you buy a single seat, ask whoever manages your licenses what you are already paying for. Second, the free tier does not let people share with each other, so a real team of any size ends up on Pro at minimum.

For a company with, say, 40 people who open dashboards, Power BI pricing at the Pro tier is 40 times $14, or about $560 a month. That is a rounding error next to a payroll. So if the license is the whole conversation, you are looking at the wrong number.

Do you need Power BI Premium Per User?

Power BI Premium Per User costs $24 a month and buys the report builders more room to work. Bigger data models, refreshes that run more often, and paginated reports for the kind of pixel-perfect statements finance sends out. You do not put your whole company on it. You put the handful of people who build and maintain the reports on it, and leave everyone who just reads dashboards on the $14 Pro seat.

The decision is a headcount question you can answer in a meeting. Count the people who build. If that is five or ten heavy builders, Premium Per User at $24 each is cheap insurance against slow refreshes and models that hit a ceiling. Count the people who only view. Keep them on Pro until the viewer number gets large enough that capacity becomes the cheaper path, which is the next question.

Where does Azure Data Factory pricing come in?

This is the part that catches finance off guard. Power BI shows the numbers, but something has to collect the data from your apps, clean it, and load it somewhere Power BI can read. That plumbing is often Azure Data Factory, and it has its own meter.

Azure Data Factory pricing is usage-based, not per seat. You pay for pipeline runs, roughly a dollar per 1,000 activity runs, for the compute that moves the data, billed by the hour, and a small monthly charge for each active pipeline. You will also see this written as azure datafactory pricing, one word, but it is the same Microsoft service. For a mid-sized company moving a normal amount of data on a daily schedule, this tends to land in the tens to low hundreds of dollars a month. It grows with how much data you move and how often, so it is worth watching, though it rarely dwarfs the rest.

The point for a buyer is simple. When someone quotes you a data project, the Power BI seats are one line, and the Azure plumbing is a separate line that keeps ticking every month. Ask for both.

What makes the bill bigger than the license?

The expensive part is the data underneath, not the tool on top. If sales and finance pull from different places with different definitions, no license tier makes them agree, and you end up paying for a reporting tool that people still do not trust.

The numbers back this up. Gartner puts the average cost of poor data quality at $12.9M a year per organization. And 67% of companies say they do not fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Drexel LeBow and Precisely, 2025). Meanwhile about 70% of the dashboards teams build go unused (RevealBI), usually because people still keep a private spreadsheet on the side. Every one of those is money spent on Power BI pricing and Azure bills that returns nothing, because the data going in was never sorted out.

So the spend that matters sits in the wasted licenses, the slow close, and the meeting where two right-looking numbers disagree and a decision gets made on the wrong one. The $14 and the $24 are the easy part.

How do you keep Power BI pricing under control?

You keep it in check with four decisions, none of which need you to build a report.

  1. Split viewers from builders. Put the few people who build on Premium Per User, and everyone who only reads on Pro. Do not buy the expensive seat for people who never open the editor.
  2. Only move to capacity when the math says so. Capacity earns its price once you have a lot of viewers. At $14 a seat, a few hundred viewers costs about the same as an F64 capacity, so somewhere north of 300 to 500 report viewers the reserved capacity starts to be cheaper, and it removes the per-seat count for readers entirely.
  3. Track the Azure meter, not just the seats. Put the Azure Data Factory and storage bill on the same page as the licenses so nobody forgets the plumbing keeps charging.
  4. Give one person the numbers. One owner in finance or operations who protects a single definition per metric. This is the cheapest control you have, and it is the one most companies skip.

Here is the one part that goes a level deeper, kept short. The capacity choice is a break-even sum. Take your total viewer count, multiply by $14, and compare it to the monthly price of an F64 capacity, which sits around $5,000 on an annual reservation and more if you pay by the hour. Below the line, per-seat Pro is cheaper. Above it, capacity wins and stops you counting readers one by one. You do not need to run this yourself. You need to know it exists and ask whoever is quoting you to show you the number at your headcount.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Power BI cost per user? Power BI Pro is $14 per user a month, and Power BI Premium Per User is $24 per user a month. The free tier is $0 but does not let people share reports with each other, so any real team lands on Pro at minimum.

What is the difference between Power BI Pro and Premium Per User? Pro at $14 lets people publish and open shared reports. Power BI Premium Per User at $24 adds larger data models, more frequent refreshes, and paginated reports. You give the $24 seat to the people who build reports and keep everyone else on Pro.

When is Power BI capacity cheaper than per-user licensing? Once you have a large number of viewers. Multiply your viewer count by $14 and compare it to the monthly price of an F64 capacity, which is around $5,000 on an annual reservation. Past roughly 300 to 500 viewers, the fixed capacity tends to cost less and removes the per-reader seat count.

Does Power BI pricing include the cost of moving my data? No. Getting data into Power BI is often a separate Azure Data Factory pricing line, billed by usage rather than per seat. It usually runs in the tens to low hundreds of dollars a month for a mid-sized company, and it keeps charging every month, so ask for it as its own number.

Do we already pay for Power BI without knowing it? Often, yes. Power BI Pro is bundled inside some Microsoft 365 plans. Before you buy new seats, ask whoever manages your Microsoft licenses what your current plan already covers.

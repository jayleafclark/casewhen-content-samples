Why Is Our Month End Close So Slow? The Real Causes

Quick Answer: Why is our month end close so slow usually comes down to three things: your team rebuilds the same reports by hand every month, one person owns the numbers nobody else can reproduce, and the figures from different tools never agree on the first pass. Fix the data model underneath the close so numbers tie out automatically, and the days you lose to re-entering and reconciling go away.

Every month your team loses the same days to the same scramble, and you are left asking why is our month end close so slow when you already bought the tools that were supposed to speed it up. The reports still get built by hand. Someone still pulls an export, pastes it into a workbook, and checks it against last month by eye. The close does not drag because your people are slow. It drags because the work underneath the close was never set up to run itself.

This is the pattern we see in almost every finance team that calls us. The software is fine. Power BI is installed, the ERP is running, the data is technically all there. But the close still waits on manual steps that happen the same way every single month, and nobody has ever been given the time to remove them.

Why is our month end close so slow when we already have the tools?

Here is the honest version. Buying Power BI or a new ERP does not close your books. It gives you the pieces. The close is slow because the pieces were never connected into one trusted flow, so your team fills the gap with manual work.

A typical close has four hidden bottlenecks:

  • Manual exports. Someone downloads a report from the ERP, opens Excel, and rebuilds the same summary they rebuilt last month. This step alone eats days across a team.
  • One-person knowledge. The reconciliation logic lives in one analyst's head and one giant spreadsheet. When that person is on leave, the close stalls.
  • Numbers that do not reconcile. Revenue in the CRM says one thing, revenue in the ERP says another, and finance spends a day finding out which is right before anything can be signed off.
  • Late adjustments. A journal entry lands on day four, and every downstream report has to be rebuilt by hand because nothing refreshes on its own.

None of these are people problems. They are all setup problems, and setup problems are fixable.

What actually slows the close down each month?

The single biggest time sink is the data gathering that happens before anyone can even start the accounting. The days go there, well before the first journal entry.

Finance teams routinely spend more of the close pulling, pasting, and cross-checking data than they spend analyzing it. According to the AFP and APQC, FP&A teams spend just 25 percent of their time on analysis and 42 percent of it gathering data (AFP and APQC, 2019). That gap is the close problem in one line. The people you hired to interpret the numbers are stuck assembling them instead.

When the data gathering is manual, every month starts from zero. Nothing carries over. The workbook someone built in July has to be rebuilt in August because the source data moved, a column changed, or a new account was added. So you never really have one slow month. You have the same slow month repeated, because the work was never made repeatable.

How do manual Excel exports make the close slower?

Excel is not the villain here. The problem is what the exports hide.

When your close depends on someone downloading a file and reshaping it, three risks stack up. First, the number can be wrong and nobody will catch it, because there is no automatic check against the source. Second, the process only works while that one person is available to run it. Third, every export is a fresh chance for a bad formula or a mis-pasted range to slip a wrong figure into the final pack.

Research on real business spreadsheets found that 94 percent of the workbooks audited contained errors. When your month end sign-off rests on hand-built workbooks, you are trusting a process that has been shown, again and again, to carry mistakes. A wrong figure that reaches an investor update or a bank covenant report gets walked back in front of the people you least want to correct yourself in front of.

The fix is to move the repeated, rule-based work out of Excel and into a modeled layer that produces the same numbers the same way every month, so your team uses Excel for judgment instead of assembly.

What does a fast close actually look like?

A fast close is boring, and that is the point. The numbers are ready because they were built continuously through the month, not assembled in a panic on day one.

Here is the difference in plain terms.

The slow closeThe fast close
Reports rebuilt by hand each monthReports refresh automatically from one model
Reconciliation lives in one person's headReconciliation logic is documented and repeatable
CRM and ERP numbers argue until someone checksOne agreed number, defined once, used everywhere

Top-performing finance teams close the month in five business days or fewer, while the slowest take ten or more (APQC, 2023). The teams at the fast end are almost never faster at accounting. They have simply removed the manual steps between the source data and the finished report, so the days are spent reviewing rather than rebuilding.

How do you make the close reconcile on the first pass?

The reconciliation fight, where the CRM and the ERP show two different numbers, is almost always a data modeling problem. The accounting is usually correct on both sides, and the two figures were simply defined differently.

When revenue is defined one way in the CRM and a slightly different way in the ERP, both numbers are technically correct and they will never match. The fix is to define each key figure once, in a single semantic model that sits between your source tools and your reports. Revenue means one thing. A customer is counted one way. A period closes on one calendar. Every report, and every person, then pulls from that one definition instead of re-deriving it.

Once that model exists, reconciliation stops being a manual hunt. The numbers tie out because they came from the same place. This is the layer most finance teams are missing, and it is the layer that turns a ten-day close into a five-day one.

What can a controller change first?

You do not have to rebuild everything to get the close back. Start with the one report that eats the most days, and ask a simple question: which steps happen the exact same way every month? Those steps are the ones to automate first, because they carry no judgment and all of the delay.

Map the manual handoffs. Write down who touches each number, in what tool, in what order. The bottleneck usually becomes obvious the moment it is on paper, and it is almost always a single export or a single reconciliation that everything else waits on. That is where the first fix goes.

FAQ

How many days should a month end close take? Strong finance teams close in five business days or fewer, while the slowest take ten or more (APQC, 2023). The difference is rarely the accounting. It is how much of the close still runs by hand versus refreshing automatically from one trusted data model.

Is Excel the reason our close is slow? Not by itself. The problem is depending on hand-built exports for numbers that get rebuilt the same way every month. Move the repeated, rule-based work into a modeled layer that produces the same figures automatically, and keep Excel for the judgment work it is actually good at.

Why do our CRM and ERP numbers never match at close? Because the CRM and the ERP each define the figure slightly differently, so both are correct and neither agrees. Defining each key number once in a single semantic model, and having every report pull from that, makes the numbers reconcile on the first pass instead of after a day of checking.

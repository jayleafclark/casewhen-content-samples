[0:50-2:20] VALUE BEAT 1: the audit (Chapter: Why won't my reports agree with each other?) CLAIM: "Phase one is a data audit. No building yet." Spoken: "First week, we don't build anything. We map where every number comes from. In this engagement the company ran three source platforms, an ERP, a separate CRM, and a warehouse-management tool, plus about forty Excel files people had kept by hand for years. Each one had its own definition of revenue. Sales counted a deal the day it closed. Finance counted it the day the invoice went out. So of course the totals never matched. A consultant's first job is to find every one of those definitions and write them down. We also check something most people skip. Ninety-four percent of business spreadsheets used in decisions carry errors, so we treat the manual files as wrong until we've reconciled them line by line." PROOF/DEMO: (MODALITY: screen-record) Open the three source exports side by side, then a shared tracker where each metric is logged with its source table and its conflicting definitions. The cursor rests on two "total revenue" cells showing different values for the same month. (ON-SCREEN: source stat card, "94% of business spreadsheets used in decisions contain errors, Poon et al., 2024") PAYOFF: "Takeaway one: before anyone writes a line of code, you need one written list of every metric and its agreed definition. If your consultant skips the audit, that's your first red flag." (ON-SCREEN: numbered takeaway card, "1. Get one written, agreed definition per metric first") RE-HOOK: "Once we know what the numbers should be, the next question is where to put them."

[2:20-3:50] VALUE BEAT 2: the design (Chapter: What are they going to build?) CLAIM: "Phase two is the design. Three layers, not one big table." Spoken: "Phase two is the design, and this is where a good consultant earns the fee. We don't dump all that source data into one place and point Power BI straight at it. We stage it in three layers, and people call this a medallion design. The first layer, bronze, is a raw copy of the source data, untouched, so we always have the original. The second layer, silver, is where we clean it, fix the data types, remove the duplicates, and apply those agreed definitions from the audit. The third layer, gold, is the business-ready shape, the tables your reports read from. The point of three layers is that when a number looks off six months from now, you can walk back through them and see exactly where it changed." PROOF/DEMO: (MODALITY: custom animation, CaseWhen medallion set) Data flows left to right through three labelled panels, bronze then silver then gold. Bronze shows messy raw rows; silver shows the same rows getting cleaned and deduplicated in place; gold shows a small tidy set of business tables. A rewind arrow then runs gold back to bronze to show you can trace any figure to its origin. Brand colour #1D967C on the active layer. (ON-SCREEN: label card, "Medallion design: bronze / silver / gold") PAYOFF: "Takeaway two: ask for a layered design, bronze, silver, gold. That's what lets you trace any number back to its source instead of guessing." (ON-SCREEN: numbered takeaway card, "2. Layered design = traceable numbers") RE-HOOK: "Design's on paper. Now something has to move the data through those layers on a schedule."

[3:50-5:20] VALUE BEAT 3: the pipeline (Chapter: How does the data get from the source to the report?) CLAIM: "Phase three is the pipeline. This is Azure Data Factory's job." Spoken: "Phase three is moving the data, and the tool for it here is Azure Data Factory. A pipeline is a set of steps that copies data from a source, runs it through the layers, and lands it where the reports can read it, on a schedule you set. In this build we ran it every night at two in the morning. Data Factory pulls from the ERP, the CRM, and the warehouse tool, drops each into bronze, then triggers the cleaning steps into silver and gold. If a source is down, the pipeline retries and sends an alert, instead of silently loading yesterday's numbers again. Setting this up properly is a big part of what you're paying for, because a pipeline that half-works is worse than no pipeline. People assume the data's fresh, and stop checking." PROOF/DEMO: (MODALITY: screen-record, Azure Data Factory canvas) Drag a Copy Data activity, connect the ERP source to the bronze sink, then chain the transform activities. Zoom-punch on the trigger schedule set to daily 02:00, then the monitoring tab showing green runs and one retried run flagged amber. (ON-SCREEN: label card, "Azure Data Factory pipeline: schedule + retries + alert") PAYOFF: "Takeaway three: your pipeline needs a schedule, retries, and an alert when a load doesn't finish. Ask to see the monitoring screen, not just the diagram." (ON-SCREEN: numbered takeaway card, "3. See the monitoring screen, not just the diagram") RE-HOOK: "The data lands in gold every night now. So why does the report still show two different revenue totals? That's the next phase, and it's the one that ends the argument for good."

[5:20-6:50] VALUE BEAT 4: the model (Chapter: Where does the one agreed number come from?) CLAIM: "Phase four is the model. A star schema is what makes the numbers agree." Spoken: "Phase four is the part that ends the board-meeting argument. On top of the gold layer we build a semantic model, and we shape it as a star schema. In the middle sits your fact table, the events themselves, every sale, every order. Around it sit dimension tables, your customers, your products, your calendar. Each measure, like total revenue, gets defined once, in one place, against that model. So when Finance opens their report and Sales opens theirs, they read the same definition of revenue from the same model. There's one number, and everyone can see how it's built. This is also the layer Copilot and any AI tool reads from. If the model's right, the AI gives you the right answer. If the model's wrong, the AI is confidently wrong, which is worse." PROOF/DEMO: (MODALITY: custom animation, CaseWhen star-schema set) A central fact table with dimension tables snapping into place around it, one-to-many relationship arrows lighting up as each connects. Then two report screens, Finance and Sales, both drawing a line back to the single "Total Revenue" measure on the model and landing on the identical figure. (ON-SCREEN: label card, "Star schema: one measure, defined once") PAYOFF: "Takeaway four: every metric gets defined once, on a star-schema model, so every report and every AI answer pulls the same number. This is the phase that buys you trust in the reporting." (ON-SCREEN: numbered takeaway card, "4. Define each measure once, on a star schema") RE-HOOK: "That's the build done. But a build that's right today can drift within months, so here comes the phase most people try to cut."

[6:50-8:20] VALUE BEAT 5: ownership and refresh (Chapter: Will this still work in six months?) CLAIM: "Phase five is ownership and refresh. This is the phase buyers try to cut." Spoken: "Here's the open loop from the start. The phase people try to cut to save money is this last one, ownership and reliability, and cutting it is what makes the whole thing fall over three months later. Two parts. First, refresh. As the data grows, reloading everything every night gets slow and expensive, so we set up incremental refresh, which only processes the newest data and leaves the history alone. In this build that took the nightly run from over an hour down to a few minutes. Second, ownership. Someone has to own each certified dataset, so when a definition changes, one person updates it in one place. Without an owner, six months in you're back to three teams making their own copies, and the numbers drift apart again. Poor data quality gets expensive when nobody owns it, and this is the phase that keeps your numbers reliable and your costs down." PROOF/DEMO: (MODALITY: screen-record) The Power BI incremental refresh policy pane, setting archive to store the last 5 years and refresh only the last 10 days, with the date partitions shown. Inset custom animation over it: a row of date partitions, only the newest one lighting up and re-processing while the older partitions sit greyed out. Cut to a lower-third showing the certified-dataset badge next to a named owner. PAYOFF: "Takeaway five: budget for incremental refresh and a named owner per dataset. This is the cheapest phase to cut and the most expensive one to have cut." (ON-SCREEN: numbered takeaway card, "5. Incremental refresh + a named owner per dataset") RE-HOOK: "So that's all five phases. The question that matters to you is what it changed for the team."

How Long Should Month End Close Take? A Straight Benchmark

Quick Answer: How long should month end close take? For most mid-sized companies, eight working days is a normal close, and the high performers wrap it up in under three. Where you land depends less on your team working harder and more on how clean and connected your source data is before the close even starts. ---

You're five days into the close, the board pack is due Monday, and you still can't say whether that's slow or completely normal for a company your size. That question sits behind a lot of quiet stress in finance, and most controllers never get a straight answer to it.

So here is the straight answer, plus what separates the teams that close in three days from the ones stuck at ten. None of it comes down to your team caring more.

How long should month end close take for a company your size?

There is a rough benchmark finance leaders use, and it helps to know where you sit against it. Around eight working days is a normal, acceptable close. Teams that finish in under three days count as high performers (SolveXia). Anything past ten days is where the close starts to overlap the next month's work and people stop trusting the calendar.

Here is how the tiers tend to break down.

Close lengthWhat it usually meansWhere most teams sit
Under 3 daysHigh performer, mostly on a connected pipelineTop tier
4 to 8 daysNormal and acceptable, a few manual steps leftThe average month end close
9 to 12 daysSlow, heavy manual reconciliationCommon, and fixable
Over 12 daysThe close blocks other finance workNeeds a real rebuild

So if you close in eight days, you're sitting right on the average. That's fine right up until the business grows past it and the board starts asking for numbers sooner.

Why does the close drag past a week?

The close drags for reasons that have almost nothing to do with effort. Your numbers live in separate places. The ledger sits in one platform, sales in another, payroll somewhere else, and none of them hand finance the data in the shape it needs. About 82% of organizations say data silos disrupt their workflows (ZoomInfo), and the close is where that shows up most.

So every month a person becomes the connector between those platforms. They export, paste, map account codes, and rebuild the same workbook by hand. That master spreadsheet is also where the mistakes start, and roughly 94% of business spreadsheets used in decision making contain at least one error (Poon et al., 2024). When one of them feeds the board pack, every figure in the meeting carries a small question no one wants to raise in the room.

It shows up at the top too. Only 40% of executives say they always trust their company's data (Talend), and a close built on hand-copied spreadsheets is a big reason why. The number that lands in front of the board is only as good as the twelve manual steps behind it.

Then there is the person problem. If the close depends on one experienced controller who knows which cell links to which file, the whole month end close process stalls the week they're on leave.

What does a fast close look like?

A fast close doesn't come from a heroic finance team pulling late nights. It comes from the data arriving clean and connected before day one. In a three-day close, the numbers pull straight from the source platforms on a schedule, the account mapping is written down once and reruns itself, and the metric definitions live in one place so two reports can never disagree on what revenue means.

The people are still there. They spend the close reviewing, explaining variances, and signing off, instead of assembling a spreadsheet. That is what separates the tiers. A slow close spends its days building the numbers, and a quick one spends them checking the numbers.

What separates a three-day close from a ten-day one?

Three things tend to separate the tiers, and none of them need more headcount.

First, the sources are connected. Instead of exporting a file from four platforms, the data pulls in directly on a schedule. Second, the numbers run through one shared model with agreed definitions, so net revenue means the same thing in every report and the board pack reconciles to the ledger on its own. Third, one person owns that model and every metric is written down, so the close doesn't depend on one person's memory.

You don't have to build all of this at once. Most teams get the biggest jump from the first step alone, connecting the two or three sources that cause the most manual paste work each month. Do that, and you often move from a ten-day close toward a six-day one before you touch anything else.

What should you fix first?

Start by timing your own close honestly. Write down how many working days it takes from period end to a signed board pack, and mark which steps are done by hand. That single list tells you where you sit against the eight-day benchmark and which step is costing you the most days.

Then fix the worst manual step, not all of them. Usually that's the data collection at the very start: the exports and the pastes. Connect those sources first and the rest of the month end close process gets shorter on its own, because every step downstream was waiting on that data to land.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good month end close time? Around eight working days is a normal, acceptable close for a mid-sized company. Finishing in under three days puts you with the high performers (SolveXia). If you're past ten days, the close is slow enough to eat into the next period, and it's worth looking at why.

Is a five-day close good? Yes. Five days is comfortably better than the average month end close and shows your data is fairly well connected. Most teams at five days can still reach three with a bit of work on the first manual step, usually the data collection.

Why does our close take longer than it did last year? Almost always because you added sources or volume without changing how the data comes together. More platforms and more transactions mean more manual steps, and the close grows with them until you connect the sources properly.

Do we need Power BI to speed up the close? You don't strictly need it. You do need some way to pull sources together, define metrics once, and refresh on a schedule. Power BI, Power Query, and Azure Data Factory are a common route because most finance teams already have the licences.

How much of the close can be automated? The mechanical parts: collecting data, mapping accounts, calculating metrics, and sending the finished pack. The judgment stays with people, so estimates, one-off adjustments, the commentary, and final sign-off all remain in human hands.

How fast can we realistically improve? For one report with a few clean sources, a first working version takes a few days. A full close across many platforms usually runs a few weeks, because the mapping and testing take real care. Most teams see days come off the close within the first month.

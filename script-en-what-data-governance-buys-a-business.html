# What Data Governance Actually Buys a Business (Plain English, No Jargon)

Script

[0:00-0:12] HOOK (blunt callout, buyer pain)

(MODALITY: 3-second cold-cut b-roll of two laptops side by side, each showing a revenue figure that doesn't match, then hard cut to talking head)

> You asked one question this morning and got two different answers. Same company, same day, same word: revenue. One deck says one number, the finance sheet says another, and now the meeting is an argument about whose number is right.

(ON-SCREEN: keyword card, lower-third "WHAT IS DATA GOVERNANCE FOR BUSINESS")

[0:12-0:52] PROMISE + STAKES + OPEN LOOP

(MODALITY: talking head, cutting to quick b-roll of a tense boardroom, people pointing at a screen)

> Governance gets treated as a compliance chore, so most leaders tune it out. By the end of the next eleven minutes you'll know what it buys you in plain business terms: numbers you can trust, safe access, and one agreed meaning for every metric. No policy doc, no jargon.

(ON-SCREEN: three-word promise cards animate in: "Trusted numbers" / "Safe access" / "One meaning")

> Here's the stakes. Keep winging it and you lose a board meeting to a fight about data instead of a decision about the business. Two out of five executives say they always trust their own numbers. That's Talend. The other sixty percent are guessing in the room where the big calls get made.

(ON-SCREEN: stat card "40% of execs always trust their data. Source: Talend")

> We do this for BI teams in Berlin most weeks, so this comes from real companies, not theory. Stay to the end and I'll tell you the one thing that decides whether people trust a number, and most teams get it backwards.

(ON-SCREEN: open-loop teaser card "1 thing = trust or chaos")

Then a hard cut into the definition.

[0:52-2:15] VALUE BEAT 1, What governance is, in plain words

(MODALITY: custom animation, a single number "€4.2M revenue" sits in the middle, three arrows point in: a name tag "who owns it", a lock "who sees it", a label "what it means")

> CLAIM: Data governance is three simple rules for every important number in your business. Who owns it, who can see it, and what it means. That's the whole idea.

(ON-SCREEN: claim card "Governance = owner + access + meaning")

> Walk through it with revenue. Who owns it means one named person decides how revenue gets counted, so there's a human to ask when it looks off. Who can see it means the right people get the number and the wrong people don't. What it means is the agreed definition, does revenue include tax, refunds, that big deal that hasn't shipped yet. When those three rules are written down and enforced, you have governance. When they live in someone's head, you have a guessing game.

(MODALITY: quick screen-record, a plain one-page doc listing "Revenue: owner, access, definition", cursor moving down each line)

> PAYOFF you can use today: pick your five most argued-over numbers. Revenue, margin, active customers, pipeline, headcount. For each one, ask your team who owns it, who sees it, and what it means. If any answer is a shrug, that number isn't governed yet.

(ON-SCREEN: takeaway card "Pick 5 numbers. Ask: owner? access? meaning?")

> Good. So what does getting this right buy you? Start with the thing you feel in every meeting.

[2:15-3:40] VALUE BEAT 2, What it buys first: numbers you can trust

(MODALITY: data-viz, a before/after bar: "meeting time spent arguing about data" shrinks, "meeting time spent deciding" grows)

> CLAIM: The first thing governance buys is a number the whole room believes, so you can spend the meeting deciding instead of debating.

(ON-SCREEN: claim card "One number the whole room believes")

> Right now, when two reports disagree, someone volunteers to go check, and the decision waits a week. Multiply that across every review. Seventy percent of the dashboards your team builds go barely used, because people don't trust them enough to act. That's from RevealBI. A dashboard nobody trusts is money you spent to make a prettier argument.

(ON-SCREEN: stat card "70% of dashboards go unused. Source: RevealBI")

(MODALITY: talking head, slow single-shot on the core line)

> When one number is owned and defined, the checking stops. The board report reconciles the first time. Sales and finance walk in with the same figure, so the conversation is about what to do, not whose spreadsheet wins.

(MODALITY: b-roll, a controller closing a laptop, relaxed, on time)

> PAYOFF you can use today: next time two reports disagree, don't referee the numbers. Ask one question. Who owns the definition of this metric. If no name comes back, you found the problem, and it isn't the tool.

(ON-SCREEN: takeaway card "Two numbers disagree? Ask who owns the definition.")

> Trust is one win. The next one is the thing that keeps your lawyers and your board comfortable: who's allowed to see what.

[3:40-5:05] VALUE BEAT 3, Safe access, so the right people see the right rows

(MODALITY: custom animation, one report, three people. The sales rep's view shows only their region, the board's view shows everything, the contractor's view greys out salaries)

> CLAIM: Governance buys you safe access. The same report shows each person only what they're allowed to see, automatically, without you building five copies.

(ON-SCREEN: claim card "One report. The right rows per person.")

> Without this, you get two bad options. Either you lock the report down so tight nobody can self-serve and everything routes through your data team, or you leave it open and hope nobody screenshots the salary column into the wrong channel. Both cost you. Eighty-two percent of companies say data silos get in the way of everyday work. That's ZoomInfo. Silos are what you build when sharing safely is too hard, so people just stop sharing.

(ON-SCREEN: stat card "82% say silos disrupt work. Source: ZoomInfo")

(MODALITY: screen-record, a report reloading as two different logins, rows changing on screen)

> With row-level security set up once, the regional manager opens the company report and sees only their region. The CFO sees all of it. The external contractor sees the summary and none of the personal data. One report, enforced by who they are, logged so you can prove it later.

> PAYOFF you can use today: list the three reports you'd panic about if they landed with the wrong person. Ask your team whether access is enforced inside the report, or whether it depends on people just not sharing the link. If it's the link, that's a gap worth closing this quarter.

(ON-SCREEN: takeaway card "3 sensitive reports: is access enforced, or hope?")

> Owner, sorted. Access, sorted. Now the sneaky one, the reason your numbers disagree even when nobody did anything wrong.

[5:05-6:30] VALUE BEAT 4, One agreed meaning, because "revenue" has three definitions

(MODALITY: custom animation, the word "Customer" splits into three glowing definitions: "signed", "paying", "logged in this month", each feeding a different chart with a different total)

> CLAIM: Most disagreements aren't errors. Two teams counted correctly using two different definitions, so both numbers are right and they still don't match.

(ON-SCREEN: claim card "Both numbers can be right. That's the problem.")

> Ask three departments what a customer is. Sales counts signed contracts. Finance counts who's paid. Product counts who logged in this month. Three honest people, three real numbers, one word. Now put those in a board report and watch the meeting derail. And when the numbers keep disagreeing, people stop opening the reports at all. On average only twenty-five percent of employees use the BI tools their company pays for. That's BARC. A big slice of that is reports nobody trusts, because the definitions underneath were never agreed on.

(ON-SCREEN: stat card "Only 25% of employees use the BI tools they pay for. Source: BARC")

(MODALITY: talking head, slow delivery on the fix)

> Governance forces the boring conversation up front. You get everyone in a room and you agree, for this business, a customer means this. Then that one meaning gets built into the reporting once, so every chart, every export, and every answer the tools give back uses the same rule.

(MODALITY: b-roll, a whiteboard with "CUSTOMER = paid + active" circled)

> PAYOFF you can use today: run this test in your next leadership meeting. Ask three people, in the room, to define your single most important metric. If you get three answers, you just found why your reports don't reconcile, and it took ninety seconds.

(ON-SCREEN: takeaway card "Ask 3 people to define your top metric. Count the answers.")

> That's what governance buys, from the outside. Once, quickly, let me show you what your team does on the inside to deliver it, then I'll point you to who does it for you.

[6:30-8:00] VALUE BEAT 5, The one signposted "what your team does" beat

(MODALITY: custom animation, a simple pipeline: raw data on the left flows into a governed middle layer, then into clean reports on the right; five labeled stops light up in order)

> This is the one technical minute, and then we're back to business. Here's what a data team puts in place so those three promises stand up. Five moving parts.

(ON-SCREEN: numbered list builds as he speaks: "1 Catalog · 2 Ownership · 3 Definitions · 4 Access · 5 Monitoring")

> One, a catalog, a plain list of every report and dataset so people can find the right one instead of guessing. Two, ownership, a named person on each important dataset, so it doesn't go stale when someone leaves. Three, definitions built into one governed model, so revenue and customer mean the same thing everywhere, including when Copilot or any AI tool answers a question. Four, access rules enforced inside the reports with row-level security, logged for your auditors. Five, monitoring, so a refresh going wrong pages a person before it pages your CFO in a meeting.

(MODALITY: quick screen-record montage, a lineage view showing where a number comes from, an owner field, a refresh history going green)

> None of this is a fifty-page policy. It's five habits, wired into the tools you already pay for, Power BI, Fabric, Azure. Most teams have the tools and never switched the governance parts on.

> PAYOFF you can use today: ask your data lead which of those five you have today. Catalog, ownership, definitions, access, monitoring. The gaps are your risk list, ranked, in one question.

(ON-SCREEN: takeaway card "5 parts: catalog, owner, definitions, access, monitoring. Which do you have?")

> That's the inside view. Now, why bother, what's the mess costing you.

[8:00-9:20] PAYOFF BEAT, What it's costing you, and the open loop paid off

(MODALITY: data-viz, a running counter of hidden costs adding up: hours re-checking numbers, a slow close, a wrong call)

> Here's the bill for skipping this, in numbers. Poor data quality costs the average company 12.9 million a year. That's Gartner. Ninety-four percent of businesses suspect their own data isn't accurate, per Poon. And a month-end close that should take three days drags to eight or more when nobody trusts the source, that's Solvexia, and every extra day is your finance team fixing data instead of reading it.

(ON-SCREEN: stat stack "$12.9M/yr, Gartner · 94% suspect bad data, Poon · close: 8+ days vs 3, Solvexia")

(MODALITY: talking head, slow, direct to camera, this is the open-loop payoff)

> And that one thing I promised, the thing that decides whether people trust a number. It's ownership. Not the tool, not the chart, not how pretty the dashboard is. When one named person owns each definition, people trust the number, because there's someone accountable for it. When nobody owns it, everybody invents their own version, and you're back to two revenue numbers. Most teams buy a fancier tool and skip the owner, which is why the new tool doesn't fix the arguing.

(ON-SCREEN: payoff card "Trust comes from ownership. One owner per number.")

> So that's what governance buys you. Trusted numbers, so the room decides instead of debates. Safe access, so the right people see the right rows and you can prove it. And one agreed meaning, so revenue is revenue everywhere. Three plain outcomes, one boring word.

(ON-SCREEN: recap list, all three ticking on: "Trusted numbers · Safe access · One meaning")

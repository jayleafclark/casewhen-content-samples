What Azure Datafactory Pricing Actually Costs in 2026

Quick Answer: Azure datafactory pricing is pay-as-you-go, not a flat license. You pay roughly $1 per 1,000 pipeline runs, about $0.25 per hour of data movement, and around $0.27 per compute hour for data flows, so most small pipelines land in the low tens of dollars each month. The bigger cost is the engineering behind them. ---

You can't give your CFO a firm number for next month's Azure bill, because it changes every time your pipelines run. That's what throws finance teams off about azure datafactory pricing: there's no seat price to point at the way Power BI has one. Azure Data Factory bills you by what your pipelines do, so the number moves every month and nobody can quote you a flat figure before the work starts.

That's the part a controller or CFO gets asked about in the budget meeting, and the honest answer has two halves. The published rates are small and easy to find. The real bill depends on how much data you move, how hard you transform it, and whether the pipelines were built well in the first place. Here are both halves, plain.

What does azure datafactory pricing actually cover?

Azure Data Factory charges for four things, and most of the confusion comes from treating it as a subscription. It isn't one. You're renting activity, hour by hour and run by run.

Here's the plain version of the published rates.

What you pay forPublished rateWhat it means in plain terms
Pipeline runsAbout $1 per 1,000 activity runsEach step in a pipeline counts as a run. A daily job with a few steps costs pennies a month.
Data movementAbout $0.25 per DIU-hourCopying data from a source into your warehouse. Bigger loads use more units per hour.
Data flowsAbout $0.27 per vCore-hour, 8-core minimumCleaning and reshaping data. This is the line that surprises people.
Idle pipelinesAbout $0.80 per pipeline per monthA small charge even for pipelines that sit unused.
SSIS runtimePriced by the hour on a rented engineAn always-on server for older packages, running day and night.

The first two are cheap for most companies. A daily load that pulls from a handful of sources runs a few dollars a month in orchestration and copy charges. The third line, data flows, is where the bill grows, because that engine has an eight-core minimum and it charges per hour it runs.

Why is the sticker price never your real bill?

Because the rates above only price the plumbing. They don't price the two things that move your invoice: how heavy your workloads are, and how the pipelines were built.

Take a single data flow that cleans and joins your sales data once a day. Eight cores at about $0.27 an hour is roughly $2.20 per run. Do it daily and that's about $66 a month for one flow. Sounds small. Now run six flows, several times a day, across finance, sales, and operations, and the same math lands you in the hundreds every month. Add an always-on SSIS runtime for legacy jobs and you're into four figures before anyone reviews it.

None of that shows up when you first read the pricing page. Neither does the cost that hurts most: the trust tax. If the pipelines feed the wrong numbers into your reports, the tool renders them faster, and you pay again every month in reconciliation and in meetings spent arguing about whose figure is right. Poor data quality costs organizations an average of $12.9M a year (Gartner), and almost none of that's compute spend. Only 67% of organizations fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Precisely, 2025), and a cheap pipeline that loads bad data does nothing to fix that.

How much does an Azure Data Factory pipeline cost per month?

For a mid-sized company running standard daily loads, a realistic range is tens to a few hundred dollars a month for the Azure Data Factory charges themselves. Here's how that breaks down.

  • Small setup: a few daily pipelines, light data movement, one or two data flows. Roughly $30 to $100 a month.
  • Mid setup: many pipelines, several data flows running a few times a day, moderate data volumes. Roughly $200 to $600 a month.
  • Heavy setup: large data flows, frequent runs, and a legacy SSIS engine running around the clock. Often $1,000 a month or more.

Those numbers are the compute. They assume the pipelines already exist and run cleanly. Building them is the separate cost, and it's usually the larger one. A single pipeline that pulls from two sources into a warehouse is a few days of work. A full data foundation, with sources connected, load logic written, and checks that catch bad data before it reaches a report, is a few weeks. You budget for that once, or you pay for it every month in manual cleanup.

What surprises show up on the Azure bill?

Three things catch buyers out, and all three are avoidable if someone watches them.

First, data flows left running longer than they need to. The eight-core minimum means an oversized or slow flow burns money the whole time it runs. A tuned flow finishes in minutes. A sloppy one crawls for an hour and charges you for the hour.

Second, always-on integration runtimes. An SSIS runtime or a self-hosted engine bills for every hour it's switched on, whether or not a pipeline is using it. Companies routinely pay for engines that sit idle overnight and on weekends.

Third, pipelines that keep running for reports nobody reads. Around 70% of dashboard work goes unused (RevealBI), and the pipelines feeding those dead dashboards still cost money on every run. You're paying to refresh data that nobody looks at.

How do you keep azure data factory pricing under control?

Start with a plan, not a pricing calculator. The Azure Data Factory bill follows how your data flows are designed, so the design is where you control the cost.

Do four things. Right-size your data flows so they finish fast instead of idling on eight cores. Switch off integration runtimes when they aren't needed, or move legacy SSIS jobs onto cheaper managed pipelines. Retire pipelines that feed reports no one uses. And put a check at the end of each load so bad data gets caught in the pipeline, not in a management report.

That last one is the one that protects the numbers. This plumbing feeds your month-end close, and a close should take three days for a strong finance team, not the eight-plus days that's still common (Solvexia). A clean, well-built pipeline shortens the close. A brittle one drags it out and adds the reconciliation cost back on top of the compute.

Where does Power BI fit on top of this?

Azure Data Factory is the plumbing. Power BI is the tap leadership actually sees. The two get budgeted together, so it helps to know the layer above.

The cost of Power BI Pro is $14 per user per month, and everyone who builds or reads a governed report needs a seat. Power BI Pro costs the same whether the underlying pipeline is clean or a mess, so paying for the seats does nothing on its own to make the numbers trustworthy. Power BI pro pricing is the visible line on the invoice, while Azure Data Factory sits underneath, moving and shaping the data those reports stand on. Get the pipeline layer wrong and no amount of licensing above it will save the report.

Frequently asked questions

Is Azure Data Factory cheaper than a flat monthly license? It depends on your workload. For light daily loads, the pay-as-you-go model is cheap, often tens of dollars a month. For heavy transformation or always-on engines, it can cost more than a fixed subscription would. You pay for what you run, so the design of your pipelines decides the price.

What's a DIU and why does it matter for cost? A DIU is a data integration unit, the measure Azure uses to price data movement. More units move data faster but cost more per hour. For most copy jobs the charge is small, around $0.25 per DIU-hour, so data movement is rarely the line that blows a budget.

Why are data flows the expensive part? Data flows run on a compute engine with an eight-core minimum, charged by the hour at about $0.27 per core-hour. A flow that runs long, or several flows running many times a day, adds up quickly. Tuning them to finish fast is the main way to control the bill.

Does the Azure Data Factory price include building the pipelines? No. The published rates cover running the pipelines only. Connecting your sources, writing the load logic, and adding validation that flags bad rows are separate work, done in-house or by a partner. That build cost is usually larger than a year of compute charges.

How is this different from what I pay for Power BI? Power BI is priced per person, at $14 a month for Pro. Azure Data Factory is priced by activity, with no seat count at all. They sit in different layers: Data Factory moves and shapes the data, and Power BI displays it. Most companies pay for both.

Can the bill spike without warning? Yes, usually from a data flow left running too long, an integration runtime nobody switched off, or a large one-off load. Setting a budget alert on the Azure cost side and reviewing runs monthly keeps surprises off the invoice.

Why won't anyone use our dashboards? You built them and they sit unused

Quick answer: If you keep asking why won't anyone use our dashboards, the reasons are almost always the same three. People don't trust the numbers, the report doesn't fit how they work, and it never tells them what to do next. Fix trust first, then workflow fit, then the next step, and dashboard adoption follows. ---

You signed off the budget. Your team shipped a tidy set of dashboards, so why won't anyone use our dashboards isn't the question you expected to be asking six months on. But here you are: the sales lead still pastes numbers into a spreadsheet before every pipeline review, and finance still emails a PDF around the week of the leadership review. The dashboards are live. Almost nobody opens them. About 70% of the work that goes into dashboards ends up wasted, with only around a fifth of it getting used (RevealBI). If that sounds like your setup, the money bought working software and a set of reports that people still don't reach for.

This piece is for the person who approved the spend and now owns the question in the room: why won't anyone use our dashboards? We'll name what's going wrong in plain terms, show what a used dashboard looks like, and give you a short list to fix, in order.

Why won't anyone use our dashboards in the first place?

Three things keep people away, and they show up in the same order every time.

First, trust. If two reports show a different revenue number, people stop believing both and go back to the spreadsheet they built themselves. Only 40% of executives say they always trust their data (Talend), and 67% of organizations say they don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions (Precisely, 2025). A dashboard competes with that doubt every time someone opens it.

Second, workflow fit. A report that lives in a tab nobody has open during the meeting might as well not exist. If the pipeline review runs off a shared screen and the dashboard isn't on it, the sales team will keep pasting figures into a deck. 82% of people say data silos get in the way of their work (ZoomInfo), and a dashboard that sits outside the daily flow is one more silo.

Third, no clear next step. A page full of charts that looks impressive but never says what to do gets admired once and skipped after that. People come to a report with a question. If it doesn't answer that question in a few seconds, they leave.

What's it costing you when the dashboards sit unused?

The license fee is the small part. The bigger cost sits in the decisions people make without the report, or on the wrong number.

Think about the last leadership review where two slides disagreed and the room spent twenty minutes arguing about whose figure was right instead of what to do. That's the cost. Think about a month-end close that drags because finance is rebuilding numbers by hand instead of trusting the model. A close of eight days is considered acceptable, and the strongest finance teams close in under three (SolveXia). Every day past that's people fixing data instead of using it. Poor data quality costs the average organization 12.9 million dollars a year (Gartner), and a large slice of that's the rework that happens when nobody trusts the dashboard.

What does a dashboard people trust and use look like?

A used dashboard has three things going for it that an ignored one doesn't.

The numbers reconcile. There's one certified model behind the report, so the revenue on the sales page matches the revenue finance reports to leadership. People stop checking it against their own spreadsheet because they've watched it match, month after month.

It sits where the work happens. The report is on the screen in the pipeline review, embedded in the tool the team already opens, or sent as a short summary the morning of the meeting. Nobody has to go looking for it.

It points to a decision. Each view answers one question and shows the next step. A regional manager opens it and sees which accounts slipped, not forty charts to sort through.

How do you get dashboard adoption back on track?

Getting dashboard adoption back starts with the thing that breaks trust. Here's the order we use with clients, and it matches the order people quit.

  1. Pick the one number that has to be right. Usually it's revenue, margin, or pipeline. Agree on a single definition and put it in one certified model. When people watch that number match across reports for a few months, they stop keeping their own copy. This is the base of Power BI adoption, because nobody adopts a tool they don't believe.
  1. Put the report where the decision gets made. If the weekly review happens on a shared screen, the dashboard goes on that screen. If a manager lives in their inbox, send a short summary there. Meet people where they already are instead of asking them to pick up a new habit.
  1. Make each view answer one question and show a next step. Cut the vanity charts. A page that says "these five accounts are at risk, and here's who owns each" gets used. A page with forty tiles gets a screenshot and nothing else.
  1. Name an owner for every report. One person, not a team alias, who's responsible when a number looks off. Reports with no owner drift, and drift is where trust goes.
  1. Watch what people do, then cut what they ignore. Turn on usage metrics. If a report has three views a month, retire it or fix it. Self-service bi adoption grows when the set is small and every report earns its place, not when you publish more.

What should your team fix first?

If you want the one move that changes the most, it's the certified model behind your top three reports. This is the part your data team goes deep on, and it's worth signposting what they'll do. They consolidate the different versions of revenue into one semantic model, define each measure once in that model, mark it as certified so people see the badge, and set row-level security so each person sees only their own rows. That's a week or two of focused work, and it's the jump that ends leadership-meeting argument. Everything else in the list matters, but it earns you less until the numbers reconcile.

Frequently asked questions

Why won't anyone use our dashboards even though we spent a lot on them? Spend doesn't create trust. If the numbers don't reconcile or the report doesn't match how people work, they'll go back to their spreadsheet no matter what you paid. Fix trust and workflow fit first, and dashboard adoption follows.

How do we get people to trust the numbers again? Put one certified model behind your top reports so the same figure shows up everywhere. When people see it match their own checks for a few months, they stop keeping private copies.

Is low dashboard adoption a training problem? Sometimes, but usually not. Training helps once the reports are trustworthy and useful. If people don't believe the numbers, more training won't move them.

How long does it take to turn adoption around? The first real change, one certified model behind your top three reports, takes a week or two. Habits shift over the following months as people watch the numbers match up.

Should we just build fewer dashboards? Often yes. A small set of trusted reports beats a large set nobody opens. Turn on usage metrics, keep what gets used, and retire the rest.

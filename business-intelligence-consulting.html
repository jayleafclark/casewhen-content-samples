Business Intelligence Consulting: The Questions Nobody Asks Before Hiring One

Quick answer

Business intelligence consulting is paid, outside help that turns your scattered data into reports your leadership can trust and act on. A good firm fixes the model under your numbers, builds dashboards people open and use, and sets rules so one metric means one thing across the company. You're buying correct numbers and faster decisions, not slideware.

Most companies shopping for business intelligence consulting open with the wrong question. They ask what a firm charges per day. The number that should worry you is a different one. 67% of organizations don't fully trust the data they use to make decisions, per Precisely's 2025 Data Integrity report. If your reports sit in that 67%, a cheaper consultant who ships more dashboards just adds to the pile of reports nobody believes.

So this piece skips the sales page. It gives you the questions a controller, CFO, or head of data should ask before signing, and what a good answer sounds like. Read it and you'll brief a firm like someone who knows where the bodies are buried.

What does a business intelligence consulting firm do?

Three things, in order of how much they matter to you.

First, the model. Underneath every dashboard is a data model that decides what "revenue" or "active customer" means. When two reports disagree on the same number, the model is almost always where it went wrong, well before the chart. A business intelligence consulting firm's first job is to fix that layer so people and Copilot both return one correct figure.

Second, the reports. Once the numbers are right, the firm builds dashboards your team opens on a Monday morning and makes calls from. This is where most in-house builds stall. Around 70% of dashboard work goes unused, per RevealBI, because it answers a question nobody asked or shows a number nobody trusts.

Third, the rules. Who can see which rows, how definitions get agreed, how a new metric gets added without breaking the old ones. Boring, and it's the part that keeps your reporting alive after the consultant leaves.

If a firm only wants to talk about the pretty dashboards, that's your first warning. The dashboard is the last 20% of the work.

How much does business intelligence and consulting cost?

Straight answer: it depends on how deep the mess goes, and any firm that quotes you a price before seeing your data is guessing.

Business intelligence and consulting engagements usually land in three shapes. A fixed-scope project (fix the model, ship a set of reports) runs as one quoted number tied to a defined outcome. A day rate covers ongoing or open-ended work. A monthly retainer covers a team that owns your reporting over time.

Here's the cost most buyers miss. Gartner puts the price of poor data quality at $12.9 million per year for the average organization. Add the meetings your people spend arguing about whose number is right, and the "expensive" consultant starts to look like the cheap option. You're paying a firm to stop the wrong numbers from ever reaching a decision, which is worth far more than another set of charts.

Ask for the price as a fixed scope wherever you can. A firm confident in its work will name a number and a deliverable. One that only offers a day rate with no end in sight is asking you to fund its learning curve.

Should you hire a firm or build an in-house team?

Both can work. They win in different situations.

An in-house team is the right call when your reporting needs change every week and you have enough volume to keep a data person busy full time. A consulting firm wins when you need senior skill for a defined stretch, when your team is already the bottleneck, or when you've tried twice in-house and the numbers still don't line up.

The trap to watch: hiring one analyst, loading every request onto them, and calling it a data function. When that person is out, your close stalls and your board pack goes quiet. A firm reduces that single-person risk because the knowledge sits with a team.

How do you spot good business intelligence consulting companies?

Most business intelligence consulting companies look identical on a website. The same Microsoft badges, the same stock photos, the same promise of insight. You tell them apart by what they do in the first meeting.

Good ones ask about your close, your board pack, and the last time two reports disagreed. They want to see your actual data before quoting. They talk about the model and governance, not just visuals. They'll tell you when a piece of work isn't worth doing.

The weaker business intelligence consulting companies do the opposite. They quote fast without looking, lead with dashboards, promise a fixed timeline before scoping, and staff your project with whoever's on the bench. A firm that can't say no to a bad idea will happily build you a report that lies.

One more filter. Ask who does the work. Some firms sell you a senior name in the pitch and hand the build to a junior after you sign. You want the person in the room to be the person on the keyboard.

What should you ask before you sign?

Take these five into the meeting. The answers tell you more than any case study.

  1. "When two of our reports disagree on revenue, where do you look first?" A good firm says the model or the definitions. A weak one blames your data and moves on.
  2. "What happens to this after you leave?" You want a handover, documentation, and a team that can maintain it. If the answer is "call us," you're buying dependency.
  3. "Can you quote this as a fixed scope?" Watch whether they'll commit to a number and an outcome, or only a day rate.
  4. "Who exactly will build this?" Names and seniority, not "our team."
  5. "What would you tell us not to build?" Silence here means they'll build anything you ask, correct or not.

Give a firm these questions and the vague ones sort themselves out fast.

Frequently asked questions

### Is business intelligence consulting worth it for a mid-sized company?

Usually yes, if you're making real decisions on numbers you don't fully trust. With 67% of organizations in that spot (Precisely, 2025) and spreadsheets that carry errors 94% of the time in decision-making (Poon et al., 2024), the fee is small next to the cost of a wrong call in a board meeting.

### How long does a first project take?

A focused engagement that fixes the model and ships a core set of reports often runs a few weeks to a couple of months, depending on how many source setups feed it and how tangled the definitions are. Ask any firm to break the timeline into stages you can see.

### Do we need Power BI, or can a consultant work with what we have?

A good consultant meets your stack where it is. Many buyers already own Power BI through Microsoft 365, so the tool is rarely the blocker. The work is the model and the definitions underneath, which matter whatever the front end.

### What's the difference between a BI consultant and a data analyst?

An analyst answers questions inside your existing reports. A consultant fixes the layer those reports stand on, so the answers come back right and your own team can self-serve. You often want the consultant first, then the analyst.

### Can't Copilot or AI just do this now?

AI is only as good as the model it reads. Point Copilot at a stack where "revenue" means three different things and it will give you three confident, wrong answers. Fixing the model is what makes AI on your data safe to trust. That's the same work a consultant does.
